\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
Modern synchrotron and neutron scattering facilities have dramatically increased data output, generating \SI{7.7}{\giga\byte\per\second} \cite{celcer2018gigafrost}. Traditional \gls{HPC} file systems such as Lustre and \gls{GPFS} excel at large, contiguous transfers. However, they struggle when users need on-beamline feedback from thousands of small, partial reads. At the same time, collaborating teams often face network-mounted filesystem bottlenecks and the escalating capital costs of maintaining on-premises systems.

Cloud object storage (e.g., \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}) offers a compelling alternative with unlimited capacity, global accessibility, and pay-as-you-go economic models. However, naively mapping monolithic formats such as \gls{HDFfive} or NeXus onto \gls{SThree} leads to thousands of range-GETs for minor chunk accesses, inefficient metadata lookups, and unpredictable request fees \cite{durbin2020cloud}. In response, a new ecosystem of cloud-native storage models has emerged: chunked arrays (Zarr), columnar layouts (Parquet), dense/sparse array storage (TileDB), tree-based event stores (\gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}), each promising different trade-offs in latency, throughput, compression, \gls{CPU} overhead, and cost \cite{ambatipudi2023comparisonhdf5zarrnetcdf4,zeng2023empiricalevaluationcolumnarstorage,LopezGomez2023}.

While isolated evaluations of individual cloud-native formats have been conducted, a comprehensive, head-to-head comparison of \gls{HDFfive}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT} on realistic, terabyte-scale scattering datasets in \gls{AWS} remains lacking. This thesis, therefore, implements and benchmarks each format across multiple compression codecs and access patterns to produce a decision matrix that guides beamline scientists and data-intensive computing engineers.

\section{Background}
As synchrotron and neutron facilities have evolved, detector technologies and beamline instrumentation have driven data generation rates from megabytes to multiple gigabytes per second. Modern experiments frequently combine multiple detectors and scan axes, such as energy, angle, or polarization. This results in multidimensional datasets that can reach terabyte-scale within minutes, making traditional `download-then-analyze` workflows impossible during beam time \cite{celcer2018gigafrost}.

\subsection{Limitations of Traditional HPC Storage}
Historically, researchers stored beamline data on parallel file systems such as Lustre or \gls{GPFS} and accessed it through \gls{MPIIO} or batch-oriented workflows. These systems work great for large‐block I/O operations but have high overhead when serving thousands of small, random reads, typical of exploratory data slices or metadata queries \cite{narayan2010characterization}. Furthermore, maintaining on-premises \gls{HPC} arrays entails significant capital and operational expenditures, as well as network complexity, for geographically distributed collaborators (which is often necessary because experiments are conducted in only a few key parts of the world).

\subsection{Emergence of Cloud-Native Storage Models}
Cloud object storage, most notably Amazon \gls{SThree}, has emerged as an attractive alternative in recent years. Object stores provide virtually unlimited, durable storage, global namespace access, and a pay‐as‐you‐go billing model that shifts capital costs to operational expenses. However, monolithic scientific formats such as \gls{HDFfive} or NeXus, initially designed for \gls{POSIX} filesystems that rely on byte-addressable seek semantics, are naively stored on \gls{SThree}, and every small slice triggers hundreds or thousands of \gls{HTTP} range-GET requests (\gls{SThree}’s emulation of those seeks). For \gls{SThree} to emulate, namespace access leads to extra LIST/HEAD calls, each adding \(\approx\$0.01\text{--}\$0.02\) per 10 k operations \cite{durbin2020cloud}.

Researchers have introduced a new generation of cloud-native data models to address these shortcomings. Zarr and similar chunked array formats expose each multidimensional chunk as an independent object, enabling massive parallel GET/PUT operations and delivering aggregate bandwidths comparable to \gls{HPC} filesystems when chunks are set to a few megabytes each. \cite{moore2023ome}. Columnar storage formats such as Apache Parquet organize data in row groups and column stripes, pruning unneeded fields to reduce I/O usage \cite{ivanov2020impact}. TileDB provides dense and sparse array storage with tiled chunking and object-store backends, enabling parallel range reads/writes and reducing metadata and small-read overheads. In parallel, the high-energy physics community has extended \gls{ROOT}’s \gls{TTree} and \gls{RNTuple} abstractions to object-store backends, preserving branch-level selective reads while retaining local filesystem performance characteristics \cite{abs-2107-07304}.

Each approach offers distinct trade‐offs in latency, throughput, compression efficiency, \gls{CPU} overhead, and cost. Although individual formats have been benchmarked in isolation or within specific domains, there is no comprehensive, side‐by‐side evaluation of realistic, terabyte‐scale scattering datasets in \gls{AWS}. Addressing this gap will inform both beamline scientists, seeking responsive, cost‐effective data access, and data‐intensive computing engineers, designing next‐generation, cloud‐native scientific platforms.


\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}
Handling large-scale scattering datasets on \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree} reveals several limitations in current data platforms. First, partial reads of beamline slices, such as retrieving a single detector channel or angular data segment, can result in high latency due to the need to perform hundreds, if not thousands, of GET operations and metadata lookups, leading to delayed experimental feedback. Secondly, existing prior prototype evaluations have focused on moderate‐scale workloads. There is limited empirical data on true terabyte-scale (1~TB+) scattering experiments. This thesis benchmarks each format on a single \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} detector dataset (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames) to uncover performance, request behavior, and data ingestion considerations. Thirdly, the interaction between storage formats and compression algorithms (e.g., zstd, LZ4, gzip) has not been systematically evaluated, making it unclear which format codec combinations best optimize latency and throughput for large-scale \gls{SThree}-based workflows.

These industrial challenges raise questions for the data-intensive computing community: How can we systematically leverage and compare different storage backends to generate guidance for scientific users? Which combinations of performance, and integration trade-offs matter most when designing cloud-native workflows for interactive, partial-retrieval use cases? \emph{How do five candidate formats (\gls{HDFfive}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}) compare on \gls{AWS} regarding performance, scalability, and compression efficiency for partial-retrieval workloads on large-scale scattering datasets?}. Answering this question will enable the development of best practices for data handling at synchrotron and neutron facilities and provide transferable insights to other big-data science domains, improving experimental productivity and resource efficiency, especially in cloud computing.

\subsection{Original problem and definition}
This thesis defines the original industrial problem as optimizing the retrieval of small, targeted data slices (e.g., a single angle’s worth of diffraction frames) from multi-terabyte scattering datasets stored on \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}. “Partial retrieval” refers to reading only the necessary object segments by byte range or object key rather than downloading entire files. This scope excludes batch transfers or on-premises filesystem solutions. It focuses solely on cloud-native, object-store workflows.


\subsection{Scientific and engineering issues}
Generalizing beyond the narrow industrial case leads to several key research questions:

\begin{itemize}
 \item Which access patterns (e.g., small random reads vs.\ large sequential scans, metadata index scalability) stress each format’s architecture the most?
 \item How do different compression algorithms (zstd, LZ4, gzip) interact with object-stored data, and how does it affect end-to-end latency and throughput?
\end{itemize}

These questions are directly relevant to ongoing research in \gls{HPC} and data-intensive computing, where cloud adoption continues to challenge traditional storage paradigms.




\section{Purpose}
The purpose of this thesis is twofold. First, this study seeks to advance the scientific and engineering understanding of cloud‐native storage paradigms within the data‐intensive computing community. Drawing on design–science research methodologies \cite{hevner2004design}, it will experimentally quantify the trade‐offs among compression efficiency (zstd, LZ4, gzip), performance of retrieval, and economic metrics (request fees, egress charges) for a \(\sim\)\SI{20}{\gibi\byte} multidimensional detector dataset, with scaling implications. The decision matrix and best‐practice recommendations will inform beamline scientists seeking rapid feedback during experiments and guide platform architects in building next‐generation, cloud‐optimized scientific workflows.

Secondly, it aims to provide Scatterin AB with a practical, evidence-based framework to optimize its cloud-based data platform for large‐scale synchrotron and neutron scattering experiments. By implementing and benchmarking five distinct storage approaches (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) on \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}, this work will deliver concrete guidance on how to minimize latency for partial‐read use cases, maximize throughput, and control pay‐as‐you‐go costs under varying access patterns.



\section{Goals}
This paper aims to develop a comprehensive, experimentally validated decision framework that guides the selection and configuration of cloud‐native storage solutions for large‐scale \gls{AWS} synchrotron and neutron scattering datasets. To achieve this, the following sub‐goals are defined:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Prototype Development}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Ingest a single \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} dense 3D detector stack (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames) into each of five storage backends: \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}. No separate tabular dataset was benchmarked.
    \item Implement consistent chunking and compression (zstd, LZ4, gzip) to ensure fair comparisons across formats.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Quantitative Benchmarking}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Measure read latency for two patterns: full scan and random single‐frame reads.
    \item Record compressed object sizes in \gls{SThree} for each codec and format.
    \item Estimate request from \texttt{GetRequestsSum} and \texttt{BytesDownloadedSum}. Egress charges are out of scope.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Decision Matrix and Guidelines}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Synthesize results into a matrix correlating workload patterns (partial vs.\ bulk reads, compression) with format‐specific performance, and ease of integration.
    \item Formulate best‐practice recommendations for beamline scientists and data‐intensive computing engineers.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}




\section{Research Methodology}

We use an \emph{experimental benchmarking} approach within a design–science framework to evaluate four timed storage backends on \gls{AWS} (HDF5/HSDS, Zarr, TileDB, ROOT/RNTuple). Parquet is included \emph{pilot-only} (no timed runs).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data \& environment}:
  one dense detector stack (\(\sim\)20\,GiB, \(\sim\)1{,}300 frames, uint16) and one EC2 client reading from a co-located \gls{SThree} bucket.
  \item \textbf{Workloads \& execution}:
  two patterns, full scan and random single-frame reads (\(\sim\)15.82\,MiB/frame). Per cell (format\(\times\)codec\(\times\)pattern) run 5 warm-ups then \(n{=}50\) timed reads in a \emph{separate session} with randomized order (fixed seed). Logged: wall-clock time and CloudWatch \texttt{GetRequestsSum}, \texttt{BytesDownloadedSum}, \texttt{FirstByteLatency\{Avg,p95\}}, \texttt{TotalRequestLatency\{Avg,p95\}}.
  \item \textbf{Analysis (summary)}:
  times on the log scale; report geometric-mean (GM) time with dependence-robust 95\% CIs via moving-block bootstrap, and $p_{95}$ with the same bootstrap; for scans, GM throughput (GiB/s).
  Cross-format contrasts are \emph{descriptive, session-conditional}.
  \item \textbf{Outputs \& reproducibility}:
  compression (stored bytes, ratio), request intensity (GETs/GiB), and decision-matrix summaries for selection. Code, seeds, and a small data subset are published for reproduction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Delimitations}

This thesis focuses exclusively on cloud-native, object-store–based workflows within the \gls{AWS} ecosystem. Specifically, five storage backends are evaluated, \gls{HDFfive}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}, by ingesting and benchmarking two representative datasets (tabular and dense arrays) on \gls{ECtwo} instances with \gls{SThree} storage. The following boundaries apply:

\begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Cloud provider scope:} Only \gls{AWS} services (\gls{SThree}, \gls{ECtwo}) are considered. No experiments involve Azure, Google Cloud, or on-premises \gls{HPC} clusters.
        \item \textbf{Storage formats:} The study is limited to the five specified formats. Other emerging or domain-specific formats (e.g., Avro, \gls{ORC}, \gls{CBF}, NeXus variants) fall outside the scope.
 	\item \textbf{Workload types:} Benchmarks cover a single 3D dense detector dataset of \(\sim\)\SI{20}{\gibi\byte} with \(\sim\)1{,}300 frames.
        \item \textbf{Access patterns:} Evaluation targets full-dataset scans and random single-frame reads (\(\sim\)\SI{15.82}{\mebi\byte} per frame). This study excludes other patterns (e.g., random single-element lookups and real-time streaming ingestion).
        \item \textbf{Compression algorithms:} This study applies three widely used codecs (zstd, \gls{LZfour}, and gzip) and does not compare other options such as Snappy, Brotli, bzip2, ZFP.
\end{itemize}

These delimitations ensure a focused, reproducible comparison of core performance and cost trade-offs for large-scale scientific data storage in \gls{AWS}.



\section{Structure of the thesis}

This thesis is organized into seven main chapters, followed by appendices:

\begin{description}
  \item[Chapter 1: Introduction] Presents the motivation, scope, research question, methodology overview, delimitations, and outlines the thesis structure.
  \item[Chapter 2: Background] Reviews scientific data challenges in synchrotron and neutron facilities, surveys traditional \gls{HPC} I/O and emerging cloud‐native formats (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}), and identifies gaps in existing benchmarks.
  \item[Chapter 3: Research Methodology] Details the experimental design: dataset selection, \gls{AWS} infrastructure setup, ingestion pipelines, access patterns, performance metrics, and statistical tests.
  \item[Chapter 4: Implementation] Describes prototype implementations for each storage backend, reproducible chunking and compression, and automation of ingestion and test workflows.
  \item[Chapter 5: Results and Analysis] Presents quantitative results, latency distributions, throughput curves, compression ratios, and \gls{TCO} estimates, followed by statistical comparisons and effect‐size interpretations.
  \item[Chapter 6: Decision Matrix and Discussion] Synthesizes findings into a format‐selection matrix, discusses trade‐offs, integration complexity, and sustainability implications, and provides guidelines for beamline scientists and data engineers.
  \item[Chapter 7: Conclusions and Future Work] Summarizes key contributions, reflects on limitations, and proposes directions for extending the study (e.g., \ multi‐cloud comparisons, additional compression algorithms, real‐time streaming ingestion).
  \item[Appendices] Include detailed benchmark logs, code snippets, and supplementary figures.
\end{description}

It then contrasts legacy parallel file systems such as Lustre and \gls{GPFS} with the architectural principles of \gls{SThree} and other object stores (Sections \ref{sec:traditional-hpc-storage-paradigms} and \ref{sec:cloud-object-storage-fundamentals}). Building on that foundation, Sections \ref{sec:cloud-native-storage-models}–\ref{sec:compression-algorithms} introduce the five storage formats examined in this thesis: \gls{HDFfive} + \gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT} / \gls{RNTuple}. These file formats are matched with the three compression algorithms (gzip, \gls{LZfour}, zstd) that modulate their performance. Section \ref{sec:access-patterns-scattering} characterizes the access patterns typical of scattering analysis.



\section{Scientific-Data Context}
\label{sec:scientific-data-context}
Synchrotron light sources and spallation/continuous‐beam neutron facilities have evolved into “mega‐pixel movies“ of matter that stream raw images at gigabytes per second speeds. This section sets the stage for the storage challenge by outlining how modern beamlines generate multi-\si{\tera\byte\per\day} data, describing the acquisition hardware that drives these rates, and giving a concrete, industry example that shows why fast, selective read-back of that data matters in practice.

\subsection{Synchrotron and Neutron Facilities}
\label{sec:synchrotron-neutron-facilities}

Giant accelerator-based light and neutron sources act as kilometer-scale microscopes that let us “see through’’ centimeters of steel, polymers, or living tissue at kilohertz frame rates. By storing ultra-low-emittance electrons (or moderating proton-driven neutrons), the machines deliver beams that are \(\sim10^{10}\!-\!10^{12}\) times brighter than laboratory instruments, unlocking 3-D imaging and diffraction of virtually any material \cite{eriksson2014diffraction}. Each pulse is immediately converted into multi-megapixel images by high-speed area detectors, hybrid photon counters, \gls{CMOS} cameras, or time-pix neutron imagers. Capturing these data allows a single experiment to exceed \SI{10}{\giga\byte\per\second} of raw data.

The industrial impact is tangible. At the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, X-ray tomography has revealed how expanded-polypropylene crash foams used in car bumpers collapse under load, informing design rules for next-generation impact absorbers \cite{carnegie2024uninterrupted}. Neutron radiography visualizes sub-second water build-up that strangles polymer-electrolyte fuel cells during winter cold starts, guiding stack architecture and heating strategies for fuel-cell vehicles \cite{yoshimune2024neutron}.


\subsubsection{Typical beamline architecture}
Synchrotron and spallation neutron facilities generate intense, tightly focused beams of X-rays or neutrons that travel down a specialized experimental “beamline.” First, the beam passes through a set of front-end optics mirrors, slits, and monochromators, to shape its size, divergence, energy, and flux. Next, a precision sample stage (multi-axis translation stage) positions the specimen with sub-micrometer accuracy and may rotate it to capture different orientations. Immediately downstream, a high-speed detector records two- or three-dimensional diffraction or imaging data at rates up to thousands of frames per second.

To handle this torrent of data, a dedicated data-acquisition (\gls{DAQ}) server sits between the detector and the facility network. The \gls{DAQ} server buffers raw frames on local \gls{NVMe} \gls{SSD}s or a burst-buffer appliance, performing initial checks (e.g., dropped-frame detection, coarse image correction) before storing the data. Traditionally, the data stream goes directly into a parallel file system such as Lustre or \gls{GPFS}, making it available to downstream analysis clusters. Figure \ref{fig:beamline-journey} shows this data path into a single schematic, highlighting the fork between traditional parallel file systems and emerging object-store workflows.

  \item \textbf{High-speed \gls{CMOS} cameras} for tomography, such as the GigaFRoST system at the Swiss Light Source, sustain continuous streaming at \SI{7.7}{\giga\byte\per\second} directly to backend servers \cite{celcer2018gigafrost}.

Modern beamlines already saturate local networks with sustained data streams of $\mathcal{O}(10^{2})$\,\si{\mega\byte\per\second} per instrument. For example, the Advanced Photon Source Upgrade anticipates \SI{172}{\mega\byte\per\second} per double sector and \SI{4}{\giga\byte\per\second} facility-wide \cite{veseli2019data}. Looking ahead, diffraction-limited storage rings are projected to drive detector outputs beyond \SI{10}{\giga\byte\per\second} and data volumes of $>\!\SI{10}{\tera\byte}$ per experiment, showcasing the need for radical upgrades to storage and analysis pipelines \cite{eriksson2014diffraction}.

\subsubsection{Dimensionality of datasets}
Indexing a single 2-D detector frame by additional experimental coordinates collected during beamtime turns it into a \emph{genuinely higher-dimensional data object}. Figure \ref{fig:data-cube} makes this concrete: each 2-D image becomes one colour-coded slice in a stacked data cube, while the depth axis stores a third coordinate such as rotation angle, energy, or time. From this perspective, every pixel expands into a voxel that contains its spectrum, angular position, or temporal history, laying the foundation for true 4-D and 5-D analyses. Because modern beamlines routinely vary at least three independent axes, the raw $(x,y)$ detector image is naturally in higher dimensions, such as a 4-D (or higher-D) array:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rotation or scan angle:} a full tomographic or serial-crystallography scan may step the sample (or detector) through $10^{2}$–$10^{4}$ projections to reconstruct 3-D real-space structure or space intensity maps (\emph{rotation} $\times$ \emph{energy} $\times$ \emph{time}) \cite{app11198868}.
  \item \textbf{Energy or wavelength: } spectro-microscopy and \gls{EXAFS} experiments, the microscope continuously sweeps the X-ray energy across an absorption edge. At the same time, a fast detector captures a rapid series of images. This procedure produces hyperspectral cubes with $\sim$10–$10^{2}$ energy points per angle or time step \cite{Briois:uc5005}.
\end{itemize}

When these axes are combined, experiments routinely generate 5-D or 6-D datasets; for example, a $2048\times2048$ detector recording 1800 rotation angles, 50 energy points, and 20-time delays already contains $\sim150\times10^{9}$ pixels ($\approx$\,\SI{300}{\giga\byte} at 16-bit depth). Such “data objects’’ are significant for multivariate analyses (e.g., \ correlating oxidation state with 3-D morphology over time) but place extreme demands on storage systems. Some beamlines further append polarization, external field strength, or environmental parameters (temperature, pressure, electrochemical potential), pushing data dimensionality and storage complexity even higher.

\subsubsection{Real-world automotive example}
The automotive industry is increasingly utilizing neutron radiography to visualize water and ice formation within polymer-electrolyte fuel-cell stacks during rapid cold starts. The study used an operational neutron imaging system to map liquid distribution in a Toyota single cell down to sub-second time scales, informing design tweaks that mitigate performance losses \cite{yoshimune2024neutron}.


\subsection{Experimental Workflows \& Feedback Loops}
\label{sec:experimental-workflows-feedback-loops}
Beam time at synchrotron and neutron facilities is hard to get and expensive. Experiments must, therefore, include a \emph{closed data loop} in which raw detector frames are reconstructed, inspected, and, if necessary, used to steer the scan while the sample is still in the beam. There have been multiple ways in which studies have tried to come up with a good solution with real-time feedback to combat this. A study showed that streaming projections from the beamline to an \gls{HPC} cluster could deliver slice reconstructions within seconds, enabling autonomous adjustment of exposure, angle range, or focus during tomography scans \cite{Bicer2017RealTime}.

Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility pushes this concept further: automated pipelines tied into the metadata catalog issue progress notifications and interactive dashboards to users, allowing real-time feedback \cite{wang2024accelerating}.

\subsubsection{Implications for the storage stack}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Latency}: scientists often request slices of data (quick-look radiographs or azimuthal-integrated diffraction rings). Data retrieval times must stay in the sub-second regime to keep up with the experiments.
  \item \textbf{Throughput}: full 3-D reconstruction or machine-learning inference may pull hundreds of contiguous gigabytes at the end of a scan, often includes burst bandwidths of $\ge\!\SI{10}{\giga\byte\per\second}$.
  \item \textbf{Scalability \& concurrency}: remote teams viewing the same dataset and automated QC pipelines often need simultaneous small-read streams. This often penalizes monolithic file formats that require entire files to be downloaded or incur seek-heavy range requests.
  \item \textbf{Online analysis}: real-time processing pipelines (e.g., streaming reconstruction, AI-driven feature extraction) consume data as it arrives. Storage must support continuous, low-overhead partial reads and write-through metadata indexing to feed live analytics without waiting for scan completion.
\end{itemize}

These workflow pressures dictate the performance metrics used later in this thesis and motivate the evaluation of cloud-native, chunk-addressable storage formats that promise millisecond-scale partial retrieval.


\section{Traditional HPC Storage Paradigms}
\label{sec:traditional-hpc-storage-paradigms}
For the past decades, scientific supercomputers have relied on \emph{parallel file systems}, where Lustre and IBM’s \gls{GPFS} has stood out. These systems have allowed pushing checkpoint files and simulation outputs across hundreds of disks at tens of \si{\giga\byte\per\second} aggregate bandwidth \cite{schwan2003lustre,schmuck2002gpfs}. By stripping data over many objects, storage targets and coordinating access via distributed locks became ideally matched workloads that wrote multi-gigabyte files in large, contiguous chunks.

Those design assumptions are strained when the I/O profile flips from “few huge checkpoints” to many tiny look-ups and interactive slices. A study from the Summit and Cori supercomputers shows that small transfers (<\SI{1}{\mega\byte}) still account for the overwhelming majority of read and write calls at both the file and process levels. At the same time, in large-scale \gls{HPC} deployments, metadata operations alone can account for a considerable part of the file-system requests, often saturating the metadata servers and emerging as a primary scalability bottleneck \cite{bez2022access}. As beamlines, AI workflows, and remote collaborators pile on, the gap between traditional parallel-file-system design and real-time, small-slice demands only widens.

The remainder of this section revisits how parallel file systems achieve their impressive bulk throughput and why those mechanisms falter under modern, latency-sensitive access patterns. Thus, it motivates the cloud-native alternatives examined later in this chapter.


\subsection{Parallel File Systems (Lustre, GPFS, BeeGFS)}
\label{sec:parallel-file-systems}

Lustre, IBM \gls{GPFS} (now Spectrum Scale) \cite{schmuck2002gpfs}, and \gls{BeeGFS} have dominated supercomputing storage for over two decades, consistently delivering hundreds of gigabytes per second of throughput. By striping data across hundreds of disks and coordinating access with distributed locks, these systems excelled at writing multi-gigabyte files sequentially. The same system, however, reveals hidden costs once workloads pivot to small, random reads or remote, \gls{WAN}-mounted access.

\subsubsection{Architecture and striping model}
Classic \gls{HPC} file systems split responsibility between \emph{metadata servers} (\gls{MDS}) and many \emph{object/data servers} (\gls{OSS} in Lustre, \gls{NSD} servers in \gls{GPFS}, storage targets in \gls{BeeGFS}). Files are \emph{striped} across a few to hundreds of disks in multi-megabyte chunks. Doing this allows a single sequential read or write to optimize the bandwidth of an entire system. \gls{PFS} uses token-based distributed locking to keep \gls{POSIX} consistency on a shared-disk \gls{SAN} \cite{schmuck2002gpfs}. \gls{BeeGFS} achieves similar semantics over Ethernet by separating data and metadata and scaling the service.

\subsubsection{Bulk Sequential I/O: High Throughput on Parallel File Systems}
Parallel file systems shine whenever an application issues long, contiguous transfers, checkpoints/restarts, volumes, and snapshots. NASA’s \emph{Pleiades} cluster still sustains, \(\sim\!1\)\,\si{\giga\byte\per\second} from disk for a single 512-rank IOR stream and bursts to \SI{9}{\giga\byte\per\second} when the same data are serviced out of the page cache \cite{6507507}. Modern flagships go far beyond that, where Los Alamos’ \emph{Trinity} Sonexion Lustre tops out near \SI{1.6}{\tera\byte\per\second} for a 78 PB pool \cite{osti_1367056}. Even a single E1000 \gls{OSS} inside NERSC’s \emph{Perlmutter} can saturate \SI{41}{\giga\byte\per\second} read and \SI{27}{\giga\byte\per\second} write to one \gls{OST} \cite{lockwood2024architecture}. These traits explain why parallel file systems remain the choice for bulk sequential scans and multi‐terabyte tomography stacks.

Lustre separates responsibilities between dedicated \emph{metadata servers} (\gls{MDS}) that keep inodes and directory state on one or more \emph{metadata targets} (MDTs) and multiple \emph{object‑storage servers} (\gls{OSS}) that deliver the bulk data stored on \emph{object‑storage targets} (\gls{OST}s).  Files are \emph{striped} across a configurable number of \gls{OST}s (stripe count) with a tunable stripe width so a single sequential transfer can engage hundreds of disks in parallel. The design of flagship systems such as Oak Ridge’s Spider‐3 sustains more than \SI{1}{\tera\byte\per\second} aggregate read bandwidth \cite{braam2019lustre, oral2013olcf}.

IBM GPFS employs a distributed \emph{token‑based locking} protocol to coordinate metadata and data updates across all nodes, persisting mutations in replicated journals so that failed servers can recover without full replays. This mechanism provides stronger resilience and copes slightly better with so‑called metadata “storms” than Lustre, though at the cost of a more complex recovery state \cite{schmuck2002gpfs}.

\subsubsection{Weaknesses for many-small reads}
Parallel file systems raise aggregate bandwidth by slicing every file across many Object Storage Targets (\gls{OST}s/\gls{NSD}s). When workloads flip from multi-GB checkpoints to thousands of $4$–$64$\,\si{\kilo\byte} reads the same striping logic backfires when workloads issue thousands of sub-megabyte reads. Intel’s LUG-2018 “Small File I/O” report shows that files smaller than the \gls{DoM} threshold generate disproportionately more RPCs per MiB, leading to significantly reduced bandwidth on multi-\gls{OST} Lustre systems \cite{gmitter2018smallfile}.

Even in a namespace-sharded file system like Lustre’s \gls{DNE}, a camera‐style “one-file-per-frame” workflow still concentrates its create/unlink traffic on one hot directory subtree. Production traces from 60 \gls{HPC} systems show that \SI{64}{\percent} of files are smaller than \SI{64}{\kilo\byte}. When a system gets flushed with thousands of tiny \gls{HDFfive} or JPEG files, the operations on the metadata will increase exponentially. Based on these 60 different \gls{HPC} systems, research shows that metadata operations can account for up to \SI{80}{\percent} of total file system operations \cite{9768784}.

HPC administrators often impose per-user inode quotas because millions of tiny files overload Meta Target Devices \cite{carns2009small}. Therefore, monolithic containers such as \gls{HDFfive} can be preferred on-prem, whereas object stores favor chunk-per-object layouts (e.g., Zarr).

\subsection{POSIX-Centric Scientific Formats}
\label{sec:posix-centric-formats}
Most \gls{HPC} codes write \emph{monolithic containers}, a single \gls{POSIX}-seekable file that includes raw arrays and metadata. \gls{HDFfive} popularised this approach, while NeXus adopts the same file structure with beamline-specific naming conventions. The design is perfect for bulk, sequential, local parallel file systems scans. However, the need to search through super-blocks and B-trees for every small slice makes it poorly suited to cloud object stores and interactive, many-small-read workflows, motivating the chunk-per-object formats.

\subsubsection{Monolithic container philosophy}
The Hierarchical Data Format~5 (\gls{HDFfive}) stores every dataset, chunk, and attribute in a single byte‐addressable file whose internal super-block, B-trees, and heap tables, with the help of \gls{POSIX}, seek for navigation \cite{howison2010tuning}. Nexus, the de facto standard at many photon and neutron sources, layers a domain system on top of the same \gls{HDFfive} container, retaining identical I/O semantics while prescribing beamline-specific group names and units \cite{konnecke2015nexus}. This “all-in-one” layout simplifies the file management, but couples performance to the underlying file-system block size and how well metadata caches handle random seeks.

\subsubsection{Serial and MPIIO access}
Out-of-the-box \gls{HDFfive} (used via h5py, MATLAB, etc.) issues are independent of \gls{POSIX}. Each rank that samples a \SIrange{4}{8}{\mega\byte} slice generates dozens of tiny, <\SI{64}{\kilo\byte} reads, leading to many metadata lookups. Performance tuning guides from the HDF Group note that such traffic can result in many minor accesses and that parallel file systems do not do well with small I/O \cite{Breitenfeld2022Parallel}. Parallel \gls{HDFfive} wraps the same container in \gls{MPIIO} so that all processes participate collectively. Doing so when users provide non-contiguous selections, \gls{ROMIO} gathers them into a single larger request (data sieving and collective buffering).

Even when using collective metadata I/O, each \gls{HDFfive} read still must fetch the superblock and descriptive metadata before reading the raw data. In the CCGrid’21 benchmarks on Summit and Cori, metadata I/O alone accounted for as much as ~58\% of the total read time \cite{9499420}. \gls{HDFfive}/NeXus excels for large, sequential scans and single-node post-processing. However, their single-file, \gls{POSIX} heritage makes interactive, many-small-read workloads vulnerable to metadata hot spots. These limitations motivate the chunk-per-object designs of Zarr, Parquet, and the other cloud-native formats evaluated in this report.


\section{Cloud Object Storage Fundamentals}
\label{sec:cloud-object-storage-fundamentals}
Cloud object stores, presented by Amazon \gls{SThree}, sit at the core of the storage formats benchmarked in this thesis. \gls{SThree}’s defining features are a flat, immutable-object namespace, global durability, and a pay-as-you-go pricing model. Amazon \gls{SThree}, therefore, removes local capacity provisioning constraints, but the system does introduce \gls{HTTP}S-level latency and per-request charges that do not exist in local \gls{NVMe} or parallel file systems. Grasping these trade-offs is therefore vital before interpreting the performance results.

\subsection{Architectural model}
Amazon Simple Storage Service (\gls{SThree}) organizes data in \emph{buckets} that share a \emph{flat global namespace}, and directory hierarchies are only a key-naming convention. Objects are \emph{immutable}, meaning any modification creates a new version of that object/file. Since December 2020, \gls{SThree} guarantees \emph{strong read-after-write consistency} for all operations, removing the race conditions that earlier eventual-consistency semantics introduced \cite{AWSConsistency2020}.


\subsection{Request classes and cost drivers}
Every interaction with \gls{SThree} is an \gls{HTTP}S \gls{API} call in one of four core request classes. Each call is billable in addition to storage (\si{GB}\,month) and data-egress charges \cite{AWSS3Pricing2025}.

\subsection{Latency characteristics}
Each request traverses a multi-AZ control plane and TLS handshake, giving time-to-first-byte latencies of \SIrange{100}{200}{\milli\second} for small objects \cite{AWSS3Performance2024}. In comparison, on-premises \gls{NVMe} RAID or parallel file systems typically deliver sub-millisecond metadata look-ups, and even \gls{AWS}’s own \gls{EFS} \gls{SSD} class reports $\approx\SI{0.25}{\milli\second}$ latencies \cite{AWSEFSLatency}. This gap explains why typical ported \gls{HPC} workloads feel slow and perform poorly on \gls{SThree}. \subsection{Performance-optimisation layers}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multipart uploads}: split objects ($\ge\SI{100}{\mega\byte}$) into \SIrange{5}{5000}{\mega\byte} parts that upload in parallel and resume after failure \cite{AWSS3MultipartDoc}.
  \item \textbf{Range GETs}: request only required byte spans, ideally aligned with the same \SIrange{8}{16}{\mega\byte} boundaries used during uploads \cite{AWSS3RangeGET}.
  \item \textbf{Prefix parallelism}: \gls{SThree} scales to at least \num{5500} GET/HEAD and \num{3500} write requests per \emph{prefix}. Optimally sharding key lifts can aggregate throughput to multi \si{TB/s} \cite{AWSS3Performance2024}.
\end{itemize}

These optimization strategies allow object storage to approach \gls{HPC}-class \emph{throughput}. However, the inherent network-centric part of the system keeps \emph{latency} for small, random slice reads higher than local or \gls{NVMe}-backed file systems. This constraint motivates the chunking and indexing strategies evaluated in this report.



\section{Cloud-Native Storage Models}
\label{sec:cloud-native-storage-models}
Cloud object stores such as Amazon \gls{SThree}, Azure Blob, and Google Cloud Storage deliver effectively unlimited capacity and global accessibility. But they do not come without many drawbacks, such as request latency, immutable‐object semantics, and per‐request billing. Some drawbacks clash with the low‐latency, mutable-file assumptions baked into traditional HPC formats \cite{durbin2020cloud}. To exploit cloud elasticity without prohibitive slice-read costs, a new generation of \emph{cloud-native storage models} has emerged, each rethinking how scientific arrays or tables are partitioned, compressed, and addressed in an object store.

The remainder of this section surveys five representative approaches: \gls{HDFfive} served through the Highly Scalable Data Service (\gls{HSDS}), Zarr's chunk-per-object layout, Apache Parquet's columnar row-group design, TileDB's dense/sparse array storage, and \gls{CERN}'s page-oriented \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple} format. \subsection{HDF5 + HSDS}
\label{sec:hdf5-hsds}
The \gls{HDFfive} ecosystem represents the most mature and widely adopted solution for storing complex scientific data, spanning disciplines from climate modeling to synchrotron beamline acquisition \cite{10.1145/1966895.1966900}. However, developers designed the format for low-latency, parallel file systems. When migrated naively to cloud object stores, its monolithic layout magnifies request latency and per-operation costs. To preserve \gls{HDFfive}’s rich data model while overcoming these cloud-specific bottlenecks, the Highly Scalable Data Service (\gls{HSDS}) was created as a stateless REST layer that shards chunks and metadata into individually addressable objects \cite{readey2020hsds}.

\subsubsection{HDF5 fundamentals}
Hierarchical Data Format~5 (\gls{HDFfive}) is an open-source container, data model, and I/O library designed to store extensive, multidimensional scientific data efficiently \cite{10.1145/1966895.1966900}. An \gls{HDFfive} file consists of \emph{datasets} (n-dimensional arrays) organized in a \gls{POSIX}-style hierarchy of groups. Datasets may be either \emph{contiguously} or as fixed-size \emph{chunks}. Chunking enables transparent filter pipelines (e.g., gzip, lz4, zstd) and independent partial reads/writes. At the same time, the library’s \gls{MPIIO} pathway lets thousands of compute nodes access different chunks concurrently \cite{hdf5_chunking}. These strengths made \gls{HDFfive} the standard library/system used at X-ray, neutron, and electron facilities.

\subsubsection{Cloud usage}
Placing a monolithic \gls{HDFfive} file on Amazon \gls{SThree} causes each \gls{POSIX} \texttt{read()} call to issue a separate \gls{HTTP} Range GET. Therefore, a single \SI{1}{\mega\byte} slice triggers two network round-trips (metadata + data), amplifying latency and incurring request fee costs that traditional \gls{HPC} deployments never experience \cite{hdf5_ros3}. Moreover, the file's internal B-tree indices are optimized for low-latency block storage, not for the \SIrange{100}{200}{\milli\second} object-store round-trip typical of Amazon \gls{SThree}.

\subsubsection{HSDS: a layered service}
The \emph{Highly Scalable Data Service} (\gls{HSDS}) re-implements the \gls{HDFfive} abstraction as a stateless REST \gls{API} \cite{readey2020hsds}. Internally, \gls{HSDS} maps every chunk and metadata into separate objects (\gls{SThree}, Azure Blob, \gls{MinIO}, or \gls{POSIX}) and maintains a small \gls{JSON} document that maps logical dataset coordinates to object keys. This approach allows traversing the \gls{HDFfive} “groups” and “datasets.” With \gls{HSDS}, reading or writing array slices and modifying attributes through standard \gls{HTTP} requests is possible.

\subsubsection{Operational experience}
Production \gls{HSDS} clusters are now back multi-hundred-terabyte public archives, for instance, \gls{NREL}’s 500 TB Wind Toolkit Long-Term Ensemble dataset, which users can subset without downloading entire files \cite{draxl2024wtkled}. \gls{HSDS} retains \gls{HDFfive}’s rich ecosystem yet promises proper cloud-native capabilities.

\paragraph{File-system considerations on HPC clusters}
Most Lustre and \gls{GPFS} installations cap the number of inodes a single user may allocate because millions of tiny files saturate the metadata target (MDT) servers long before disk bandwidth runs out \cite{gmitter2018smallfile}. Therefore, a monolithic \gls{HDFfive} container carries a practical on-prem advantage: \emph{one inode per file} and negligible directory pressure. By contrast, chunk-per-object formats such as Zarr can create $10^{5}$ $10^{7}$ objects for a single 1 TB tomography scan, a scenario many \gls{HPC} administrators actively discourage or block via strict inode quotas. The same characteristic becomes an asset in cloud object stores, where inodes do not exist, and latency can be hidden behind massive GET/PUT parallelism.

\paragraph{Redundant indexing and its performance cost}
\gls{HDFfive}’s internal super-block, heap tables, and fractal B-trees replicate several file-system duties to offer \gls{POSIX}-seekable random access \cite{howison2010tuning}. On low-latency local storage, this overhead is minor, yet when a single 4 MB slice is served from \gls{SThree}, it incurs two round-trips, first to fetch the B-tree node, then the data chunk, doubling both latency and request cost. Chunk-native layouts (Zarr, Parquet, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) avoid this indirection because each chunk or page is already an independent object.

\subsection{Zarr}
\label{sec:zarr}

Zarr is an open-source system that builds on the fundamentals of storing chunked, compressed $n$-dimensional arrays in a directory-type layout of small objects \cite{zarrspecv3}. The system partitions each array into regular \emph{chunks} and stores every chunk as an independent object (e.g., a file in a \gls{POSIX} system, a key in Amazon \gls{SThree}). Zarr supports compression with popular codecs such as gzip, lz4, or zstd and utilizes \gls{JSON} metadata files. The format requires no central server, making it attractive for cloud object stores where high-latency, high-throughput parallel access dominates.

\subsubsection{Cloud-native performance}
Because each chunk maps to a \gls{SThree} key, multiple parallel GETs can execute simultaneously, achieving high aggregate bandwidth. Time-to-first-byte per chunk on \gls{SThree} is typically \SIrange{100}{200}{\milli\second}; concurrency improves throughput rather than single-request latency. Write throughput scales similarly via thousands of concurrent PUTs (one per chunk), enabling real-time ingestion at modern beamlines without seeing too many performance drawbacks.

\subsubsection{Growing ecosystem}
Researchers across various fields have adopted Zarr; \gls{GDAL} provides a native driver for geospatial rasters \cite{gdal2024zarr} while the bioimaging community standardized on \gls{OME}-Zarr for petabyte-scale microscopy archives \cite{moore2023ome}. A NumFOCUS-backed governance model and a public Zarr-specs \gls{RFC} process ensure long-term stability in the system \cite{zarrspecv3}.

Zarr’s chunk-per-object design aligns perfectly with the partial-read workloads studied in this report. However, its performance depends on chunk size, sharding, and metadata unification.

\subsection{Apache Parquet}
\label{sec:apache-parquet}
Apache Parquet is an open, columnar storage format originally developed at Twitter and Cloudera to bring Google Dremel–style analytics to the Hadoop ecosystem. The Parquet format organizes a file into one or more \emph{row groups} where, within each row group, every column is stored contiguously as a \emph{column chunk} and encoded with lightweight compression and a dictionary encoding scheme. Because the system only reads the required columns and the row groups that satisfy a filter predicate, Parquet dramatically reduces I/O volume for wide, sparse tables. Parquet supports several compression codecs, such as Snappy, GZIP, deflate, and BZIP2 \cite{vohra2016apache}. Within each row group, Parquet applies column-specific encodings—such as dictionary or run-length encoding—and independent compression, further shrinking the on-disk footprint and amplifying the format’s I/O-reduction advantage.

\subsubsection{Cloud-native performance}
On Amazon \gls{SThree}, each row group is laid out as a large (typically \SIrange{64}{256}{\mega\byte}) contiguous byte range, allowing clients such as Apache Arrow’s vectorized reader or \gls{AWS} Athena to fetch gigabyte-scale slices with a single Range GET. The AWS Athena pricing page states that converting data to compressed, columnar formats like Parquet “can save up to \SI{90}{\percent} per query and improve performance” compared with raw \gls{CSV} \cite{aws_athena_pricing}.

\subsubsection{Ecosystem adoption}
Parquet has become the default on-disk file format throughout today’s analytics stack. Apache Spark uses Parquet as the \emph{default data source}, and Apache Iceberg likewise keeps its immutable data files in Parquet \cite{iceberg_spec}. In the in-memory layer, Pandas and Apache Arrow provide first-class readers/writers for Parquet \cite{pandas_read_parquet}. Primary cloud analytics services natively ingest Parquet, including Google BigQuery \cite{bq_parquet}, \gls{AWS} Glue \cite{aws_glue_parquet}, and Amazon Athena \cite{aws-athena-columnar}.

Parquet’s columnar design is perfect for the tabular metadata portion of scattering experiments, where scientists frequently select a handful of columns across millions of rows. However, its row-group granularity (tens of MB) and lack of native $n$-D chunking can inflate latency for small pixel tiles.


\subsection{TileDB}
\label{sec:tiledb}
TileDB is an open-source array storage engine for both dense and sparse data, organizing writes to immutable, timestamped \emph{fragments}. \cite{Papadopoulos2016TileDB,TileDBDataLayout}. Each fragment contains attribute data serialized into \emph{data tiles}. On object stores, TileDB uses a virtual filesystem with native backends for \gls{SThree}, Azure Blob, and GCS. On AWS \gls{SThree}, it writes using multipart uploads to create append-only fragment objects \cite{TileDBBackends,TileDBS3}. The array directory contains the schema under \texttt{\_\_schema/}, per-fragment data, and fragment metadata. \cite{TileDBFragmentMeta}.

\subsubsection{Cloud mapping and object count}
Unlike chunk-per-object layouts, a single fragment typically produces a small, fixed set of objects. It generates one attribute file per attribute, one fragment-metadata file, one commit record, and the already-existing schema file. The total object count scales with the number of \emph{fragments} and attributes rather than the number of tiles, which reduces LIST/HEAD amplification on \gls{SThree} for large arrays \cite{TileDBDataLayout,TileDBFragmentMeta}. Excessive fragmentation, however, increases first-open overhead because readers must fetch per-fragment metadata \cite{TileDBConsolidateFragmentMeta}.

\subsubsection{Reads and pruning}
On open, TileDB loads fragment metadata and per-tile statistics/min-max bounds. The system then queries the pruned fragments and data tiles whose minimum-bounding rectangles (MBRs) do not intersect the requested subarray \cite{TileDBFragmentMeta,TileDBMBR}. Reads support ordered or unordered layouts, multi-range subarrays \cite{TileDBReadLayouts}.

\subsubsection{Writes, immutability, and time travel}
All writes create new immutable fragments and commit files, enabling lock-free multiple-reader/multiple-writer behavior and consistent reads at a chosen timestamp range (“time traveling”). Fragment merging helps compact many small fragments into larger ones, which allows reclaim space \cite{TileDBTimeTravel}.

\subsubsection{Compression and filters.}
TileDB applies a per-attribute filter pipeline to tiles. Supported compressors include GZIP, Zstandard, LZ4, RLE, BZIP2, and integer-oriented transforms (bit-shuffle, bit-width reduction, double-delta). Checksums (MD5/SHA-256) are available for integrity. Arrays can be encrypted at rest using AES-256-GCM \cite{TileDBCompression,TileDBChecksums,TileDBEncryption}.

\subsection{ROOT / RNTuple}
\label{sec:root-rntuple}
\gls{ROOT} is an object-oriented C++ framework developed at \gls{CERN} to handle the petabyte-scale data streams of high-energy-physics experiments \cite{blomer2020evolution}. Its core storage abstraction, \gls{TTree}, stores events as rows and detector variables as \emph{branches}. The format splits each branch into contiguous \emph{baskets}, allowing readers to load them independently. This column-friendly layout lets analysis jobs load only the needed branches, minimizing I/O and deserialization overhead, a property directly relevant to slice-level scattering analysis.

\subsubsection{RNTuple redesign}
After two decades of incremental evolution, \emph{\gls{TTree}} showed architectural limits for modern heterogeneous hardware and cloud storage. The \gls{RNTuple} project, therefore, re-implements \gls{ROOT} I/O with a stricter columnar layout, page-based compression, and a self-describing, versioned metadata footer \cite{blomer2020evolution}. Pages (\(\approx\)\,\SIrange{8}{16}{\kilo\byte}) are grouped into clusters so that entirely columnar reads issue a single contiguous request, while sparse event selections fetch only the referenced pages. By decoupling metadata from data pages, RNTuple avoids rewriting the entire file when appending events, which is critical for streaming detectors and results in improved partial reads.

\subsubsection{Object-store back-end}
Through the generic ROOT Page Source interface, pages can reside in \gls{POSIX} files, over \gls{HTTP}(S), or as individual \gls{SThree} objects \cite{rootRNTuple2025}. Scaling studies by Blomer showed near-linear throughput up to \(\mathcal{O}(10^{2})\) concurrent readers on a >100‐core \gls{HPC} cluster served by a \gls{DAOS} object store, achieving an aggregated read rate above \SI{35}{\giga\byte\per\second} before the storage system became the bottleneck \cite{blomer2024root}.

This architecture thus provides high-throughput, scalable, and selective access to petabyte-scale datasets, whether on \gls{HPC} or in the cloud, making RNTuple a promising foundation for modern, slice-level scattering analyses.


\section{Compression Algorithms Reviewed}
\label{sec:compression-algorithms}
Lossless compression is a first-order lever for improving performance and reducing costs in cloud object–store workflows. Smaller chunks reduce \gls{SThree} egress and request fees, while faster codecs shorten the latency that dominates interactive partial reads in synchrotron experiments.

The focus is on three open-source codecs that form a spectrum of trade-offs: \emph{gzip/DEFLATE} (high ratio, high \gls{CPU}), \emph{LZ4} (low ratio, ultra-low latency), and \emph{Zstandard} (\emph{zstd}, balanced). All five backends under test (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) support gzip, LZ4, and Zstandard (built-in or via standard filters/plugins).

\subsection{gzip}
\label{sec:compression-gzip}
GNU \emph{gzip} has been the default lossless compression tool on Unix-like systems since 1992. \gls{RFC}~1952 formalizes gzip’s file format and header/trailer structure, and those specifications have remained patent-free and backward-compatible across three decades of scientific workflows \cite{deutsch1996rfc1952}.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic design}
The compressor implements the \emph{DEFLATE} scheme, a cascade of \gls{LZseventyseven} sliding-window substitution followed by static/dynamic Huffman coding tunable via nine quality levels that trade compression time for ratio. Level 6 is the GNU default, while level 1 sacrifices a \(\approx\) 30\% ratio to gain 3–4\(\times\) speed \cite{deutsch1996rfc1952}.

\subsubsection{Performance on scientific data}
Benchmarks on floating-point climate and remote-sensing datasets stored in \gls{netCDF}-4/\gls{HDFfive} show typical space savings of 1.6–2.2\(\times\) at levels 4–6. However, this did show that with compression, the \gls{CPU} costs were 2–5\(\times\) higher than LZ4, and decompression throughput that saturates a single \SI{10}{\giga\bit\per\second} core around \SI{500}{\mega\byte\per\second} \cite{delaunay2019evaluation,chen2023fcbench}.

\subsubsection{Integration with chunked formats}
Native support for gzip exists in all five tested backends. Because gzip compresses each chunk independently, its compression ratio varies with chunk size and needs to be considered during these experiments. In the experiments, Gzip is used as a “high-ratio, high-\gls{CPU}” reference, and its mature ecosystem guarantees consistent behavior across all five storage back ends. Its well-characterized latency overhead provides a clear baseline against which to measure the partial-read performance gains of newer codecs like Zstandard and LZ4.

\subsection{LZ4}
\label{sec:compression-lz4}
LZ4 emerged in 2011 as a lightweight derivative of the Lempel–Ziv (\gls{LZseventyseven}) family, designed to maximize streaming throughput while keeping memory overhead to a minimum. Its open-source framing format defines a compact header, provides optional block and content checksums, and employs a skippable frame mechanism, allowing adding new features without breaking older decoders. These attributes have led to widespread adoption in operating systems, high-performance storage engines, and scientific data formats where sub-millisecond decode latency is critical.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic profile}
The codec emphasizes extremely fast byte-wise parsing and a greedy match-finder that limits back-references to \SI{64}{\kilo\byte}. Because of this, the compression runs at $>$\SI{500}{\mega\byte\per\second} per core while decompression routinely exceeds \SIrange{2}{3}{\giga\byte\per\second}, albeit with modest ratios (1.2–1.7\(\times\) on floating-point data) \cite{lz42025,7440278}. A high-compression variant \texttt{lz4hc} improves the ratio by $\sim$15\,\% at 3–5 \(\times\) the \gls{CPU} cost.

\subsubsection{Empirical results on scientific workloads}
Almeida \emph{et al.} compared LZ4 with \gls{ZLIB} on ROOT HEP and climate grids, reporting \SIrange{1.0}{1.2}{\giga\byte\per\second} decompression (versus \SI{120}{\mega\byte\per\second} for \gls{ZLIB}) at the expense of only a 5–10\,\% ratio loss \cite{almeida2014two}. The \gls{VLDB}’24 “\gls{FCBench}” study recently measured the \gls{CPU} variant bitshuffle with LZ4 across 33 floating-point datasets. The study showed that it sustained \SI{0.9}{\giga\byte\per\second} compression and \SI{1.2}{\giga\byte\per\second} decompression on \SI{64}{\kilo\byte} blocks while achieving a median compression ratio of 1.43 \cite{chen2023fcbench}.

\subsubsection{Integration in storage formats}
LZ4 filters are first-class options in \gls{HDFfive} (\texttt{H5Z\_LZ4}), Zarr v3, Parquet, and TileDB. Because the LZ4 \SI{64}{\kilo\byte} match window limits cross-chunk redundancy, chunk sizes above \SIrange{1}{2}{\mega\byte} yield diminishing returns, which for this study will align well with \gls{SThree} request-cost sweet spots.

LZ4 represents the “low-latency, low-\gls{CPU},” serving as the low-latency reference with gzip and zstd in our benchmarks. The extent to which compression ratio can be traded for sub-second partial reads without materially increasing monthly \gls{SThree} storage costs will be quantified.

\subsection{zstd}
\label{sec:compression-zstd}
Zstandard (\emph{zstd}) was released by Collet in 2016 and standardized by the IETF as \gls{RFC}~8878 \cite{collet2021rfc8878}. It combines an \gls{LZseventyseven} dictionary back-reference engine with Finite-State Entropy (\gls{FSE}) coding. It supports window sizes up to 128 MiB and offers 23 quality levels ($-7$ to 22), optional long-range mode, and trained dictionaries (negative values activate ultra-fast modes that trade ratio for speed). Decompression speed is deliberately kept almost constant across levels, whereas compression trades \gls{CPU} for ratio.

\subsubsection{Throughput and ratio characteristics}
According to the \emph{zstd} manual, the fastest compression mode exceeds \SI{200}{\mega\byte\per\second} per core, while the decoder sustains $>$\SI{500}{\mega\byte\per\second} per core on commodity x86 \gls{CPU}s \cite{zstdman2025}.

Promberger \emph{et al.}\ measured \emph{zstd} level 2 on a 4.9 GB \gls{LHCb} collision dataset and found that its \emph{compression} throughput was roughly 2–3 \(\times\) higher than that of \emph{zlib} level 2 while achieving an almost identical compression ratio, \cite{promberger2023characterization}.

Delaunay et al. found that “Shuffle + Zstandard-2” compresses \gls{CFOSAT} and \gls{SWOT} remote-sensing files about 1.4 \(\times\) to 7 \(\times\) faster than the mission’s baseline “Shuffle + Deflate-3/4”. It delivers virtually identical space savings, accelerating decompression by up to seven-fold \cite{delaunay2019evaluation}. Across the full 33-dataset \gls{FCBench} suite, the \gls{VLDB}’24 study reports that a \emph{bitshuffle+zstd} pipeline achieves a harmonic-mean compression ratio of 1.47, while sustaining compression and decompression throughputs of $\sim\!\SI{1.4}{\giga\byte\per\second}$ and $\sim\!\SI{1.3}{\giga\byte\per\second}$, respectively  \cite{chen2023fcbench}.

\subsubsection{Integration in scientific formats}
zstd is a first-class codec in Parquet, TileDB, and available as extensions for \gls{HDFfive} and Zarr v3. With \gls{FSE} contexts resetting per block, 1–4 MiB chunks maximize the ratio without inflating \gls{SThree} request counts. Zstd occupies the “balanced ratio/latency” middle ground between gzip (high ratio, high \gls{CPU}) and LZ4 (low ratio, low \gls{CPU}) in the study.

\subsection{Small-slice interactive reads}
At many beamlines, an \emph{interactive slice} is a reconstructed tomographic plane or detector region of interest. For example, the GigaFRoST camera at \gls{SLS}--\gls{TOMCAT} streams \(2016\times1800\) px, 16-bit projections at \SI{7.7}{\giga\byte\per\second}, so a single projection (or a stack of a few tiled ROIs) naturally falls in this size window \cite{celcer2018gigafrost}. Likewise, the APS nanoprobe workflow achieves live neural-network inference on \(512\times512\) diffraction frames at \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\) (one frame every \(\SI{10}{\milli\second}\)), establishing a practical millisecond-to-sub-second latency budget for feedback loops \cite{babu2023deep}.

Partial reads are equally critical \emph{after} acquisition. For example, strain- and stress-mapping routines integrate Bragg peaks only within narrow azimuthal sectors (\(\Delta\chi\approx5^{\circ}\)) or select a handful of detector tiles, discarding the rest of the frame. High-energy diffraction microscopy at APS, for instance, reconstructs grain-resolved strain tensors by repeatedly loading just those angular wedges that intersect a given grain’s Debye–Scherrer rings, cutting I/O by an order of magnitude \cite{lienert2011high}. These workflows, therefore, see many benefits of simply loading in partial slices of data rather than complete datasets.

\subsection{Full-dataset sequential scans}
A \emph{full-dataset scan} means streaming every projection or event record, typically \(10^{2}\)–\(10^{3}\,\mathrm{GB}\) per tomographic run or several terabytes per serial-crystallography campaign. Full dataset scans, therefore, require moving data from storage to the compute tier in a single, sequential pass.

Complete reads underpin high-fidelity analyses that cannot tolerate missing frames. Iterative phase-retrieval and filtered back-projection algorithms in tomography require the entire sinogram for each refinement cycle. In serial femtosecond crystallography, clustering, indexing, and Monte Carlo integration need millions of patterns to converge on accurate structure factors \cite{maiden2009improved}. Full dataset scans are also required when, for example, doing \emph{reference re-processing} of an experiment. Tasks such as simply displaying the image in a single continuous video require loading the entire dataset. Sequential scans saturate available bandwidth, compression efficiency, and parallel read pipelines dominate time-to-solution.


\subsection{Metadata-driven queries}
A \emph{metadata query} retrieves only descriptive information, sample-environment logs, scan parameters, detector geometry, or a limited set of columns/branches without accessing the large image stacks. Typical payloads are \(\mathcal{O}(10^{1}\!-\!10^{3})\,\mathrm{kB}\), which a live beamline dashboard may issue tens to hundreds of such queries per second during an experiment.

Synchrotron and neutron facilities increasingly push raw detector frames straight into object stores (\gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}, \gls{Ceph}, \gls{MinIO}) to curb on-premise costs and enable remote collaboration. Brookhaven’s \gls{NSLSII}, for example, operates a {\small 5 PB} \gls{MinIO} cluster exposed via the \gls{SThree} \gls{API}; the Bluesky acquisition pipeline writes NeXus event documents there, and downstream notebooks read them over the \gls{WAN} \cite{Lancon2022}. The UK’s Rutherford Appleton Laboratory offers the \gls{ECHO} \gls{Ceph} object store, providing {\small 100 PB} of \gls{SThree} capacity that underpins \gls{IRIS} cloud analysis services and beamline portals \cite{ECHO2025}. In these environments, transferring even a few gigabytes across a \gls{WAN} to inspect scan headers is untenable, mainly when lightweight, metadata-only queries can satisfy the request.

\section{Summary and Link to Problem Statement}
\label{sec:summary-problem-statement}

The preceding background underscores how accelerating detector output and evolving collaboration models outpace traditional \gls{HPC} storage solutions while object storage introduces its latency-cost trade-offs. The key insights, mapped directly to the thesis problem, are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Escalating data rates at modern synchrotron and neutron facilities:} Multi-\si{\giga\byte\per\second} streams and multi-\si{\tera\byte} experiments are now routine, stressing conventional storage and analysis pipelines.
  \item \textbf{Mismatch between traditional \gls{HPC} file systems and interactive beamline needs:} Lustre and \gls{GPFS} deliver excellent bulk throughput. Still, they incur high latency and overload metadata servers when scientists issue thousands of small, random reads for on-beamline feedback.
  \item \textbf{Cloud object storage offers scale and global access, \emph{but at a cost}:} \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree} provides unlimited capacity and pay-as-you-go economics, but at the cost of introducing higher per-request latency and billing overhead, making naïve monolithic (\gls{HDFfive}/NeXus) deployments inefficient.
  \item \textbf{Rise of cloud-native, chunk- or column-based formats:} Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple} reorganize data into independently addressable objects, promising lower slice-read latency and better cost control.
  \item \textbf{Central research question:} \emph{How do \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, Parquet, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple} compare, under identical compression settings, on latency, throughput, compression ratio, and \gls{AWS} cost for partial-retrieval workloads?}
  \item \textbf{Thesis contribution and objectives:} Prototype ingestion for all five formats, quantitative benchmarking across small-slice and full-scan patterns, and a decision matrix translating results into actionable best practices.
  \item \textbf{Practical significance for beamline scientists and data engineers:} Enables near-real-time experimental feedback, reduces cloud spend, and informs the design of next-generation scientific data platforms.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Method or Methods}
\label{ch:methods}
This chapter presents the methodology to guide the benchmark study, beginning with an iterative workflow that encompasses planning, execution, analysis, and synthesis of results within a design-science framework. The experiment leverages provisioned \gls{AWS} infrastructure and synthetic datasets that mirror beamline workloads, exercising small-slice and full-scan access patterns to stress latency and throughput. A rigorous data-collection pipeline captures wall-clock time and \gls{SThree} metrics (GetRequestsSum, BytesDownloadedSum, FirstByteLatencyP95/Average, TotalRequestLatencyP95/Average), with five warm-up runs and 50 timed repetitions per case trials, ensuring reliability. Finally, a multi-criteria evaluation framework will determine these measurements into an actionable decision matrix for optimizing cloud-native storage solutions.

\section{Research Process}
\label{sec:researchProcess}
The research process for this thesis follows a structured methodology, as described in the flowchart in Figure \ref{fig:researchprocess}. The research process can be broken down into four main stages: planning the experiment, conducting the experiment, analyzing the data, and discussing the results. Each stage contributes to a comprehensive framework for benchmarking cloud-native storage formats and compression algorithms in \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}.

\subsection{Step 1: Planning the Experiment}
The planning stage involved several crucial tasks. First, the objectives of the experiment were defined, focusing on the comparison of four timed storage formats (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB, \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) with Parquet as a pilot-only artifact (no timed runs) and three compression algorithms (gzip, LZ4, zstd) concerning performance, scalability, and compression efficiency for partial retrieval of large-scale scattering data. Subsequently, appropriate datasets were selected for ingestion, using a single \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} detector dataset (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames) expanded from 80 real seed frames standard in synchrotron and neutron facilities.

Key variables, such as read latency, derived throughput, and request/byte-based outputs, are identified as suitable metrics for benchmarking. As discussed in prior studies, compression algorithms were selected based on their widespread use in scientific workflows and cloud-native environments. This stage also included the decision to utilize \gls{AWS} infrastructure for its flexibility, and scalability.

\subsection{Step 2: Conducting the Experiment}
The second step of the research process involved executing the experiment. This included setting up Amazon \gls{ECtwo} instances and \gls{SThree} Buckets.

For each format, three datasets were created—one for each codec (gzip, LZ4, and zstd). Chunking was fixed per format and held constant across runs. Two access patterns were executed per dataset: full scan of the \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} object and random single-frame reads (\(\sim\)\SI{15.82}{\mebi\byte} per frame). Logged metrics: wall-clock time and \gls{SThree} GetRequestsSum, BytesDownloadedSum, FirstByteLatencyP95/Average, TotalRequestLatencyP95/Average, allowing for post-experiment analysis. A key decision point in this phase ensured that the results were consistent and reliable. If the system detects inconsistencies, the experiment setup is adjusted for potential setup errors or unexpected issues.

\subsection{Step 3: Analyzing the Data}
This step summarizes run-level timings and S3 aggregates. Warm-ups are excluded by default, and all successful timed runs are retained. Timings are analyzed on the log scale. For each {format$\times$codec$\times$pattern} cell we report (i) geometric mean (GM) time with dependence-robust 95\% CIs via a moving-block bootstrap (MBB; $\ell\in\{4,6,8\}$; $B{=}10{,}000$; we then report the widest CI across $\ell$) and (ii) $p_{95}$ latency with the same bootstrap. The last research stage will involve evaluating the study's findings and creating recommendations for beamline scientists and data engineers. The results are thoroughly examined, considering the trade-offs between performance, and compression efficiency when working with the five different storage configurations.

Recommendations on the optimal configuration of storage formats and compression algorithms, tailored to use cases prioritizing either high throughput or low-latency access, are provided. The research will conclude with the formulation of practical guidelines for future investigations into cloud-native storage solutions for data-intensive scientific experiments.

\section{Research Paradigm}
\label{sec:researchParadigm}
The research paradigm for this thesis is grounded in an experimental research approach within the broader context of design science research (\gls{DSR}). Given that this study aims to benchmark and compare multiple cloud-native storage formats (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) where Parquet was included pilot-only (no timed runs) and compression algorithms (gzip, LZ4, zstd) under real-world scenarios, it is critical to adopt a method that highlights systematic evaluation, controlled experimentation, and result-based design. This research aims to perform a comparative analysis of storage and compression techniques and develop actionable guidelines for optimizing cloud-native workflows in the context of data-intensive scientific experiments, particularly in synchrotron and neutron scattering facilities.

The design science research paradigm is particularly suited for this study because it enables the creation and evaluation of artifacts, such as storage and compression systems used for large-scale scattering data in the cloud. Design science emphasizes the building and assessment of solutions to practical problems through the design and development of artifacts. This approach aligns well with the thesis's goal of designing a decision matrix that helps beamline scientists and engineers optimize storage formats and compression techniques in cloud environments, such as \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}.

Design science research places significant emphasis on both problem-driven and solution-driven research. The study focuses on the challenges researchers and engineers face in handling massive scattering datasets in the problem-driven component. These challenges include the high volume of data produced by modern synchrotron and neutron facilities, rapid, selective data retrieval, and the cost efficiency required for managing large datasets in the cloud. The solution-driven aspect involves implementing and benchmarking various cloud-native storage models and compression algorithms to address these problems effectively.

This research paradigm allows for an iterative experimentation cycle. For each cycle, the findings from one phase can influence the setup of subsequent tests. The decision point "Results Consistent?" reflects this iterative process, where the experimental setup may be adjusted based on preliminary results, ensuring continuous refinement throughout the research.

\section{Experimental Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:experimentalDesign}
The experimental design for this thesis focuses on evaluating and comparing four timed cloud-native storage formats (\gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}) where Parquet was treated as pilot-only (no timed runs) when applied to large-scale scattering datasets in \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree}. This section describes the methodologies used to carry out the experiments, including data collection, infrastructure setup, experimental parameters, and performance benchmarking.

\subsection{Test Environment and Infrastructure Setup}
The experiment uses Amazon Web Services (\gls{AWS}) in a cloud-native environment. Specifically, the storage backends were implemented on Amazon \gls{SThree}, and the experimental infrastructure is provisioned using Amazon \gls{ECtwo} instances for data processing and benchmarking. Figure \ref{fig:aws_topology} illustrates the overall cloud testbed, highlighting the data-flow paths and the layers used for performance.

\subsubsection{Hardware, region, and network controls}
 All runs are executed in a single AWS region with the \gls{SThree} bucket co-located. No CloudFront, \gls{SThree} Transfer Acceleration, or cross-region traffic was used. A Gateway VPC endpoint for \gls{SThree} provided private networking. The AWS SDK utilized persistent HTTP connections with a fixed connection pool, and the default retry policy remained enabled.

\subsubsection{Client libraries and access paths}
 Access used \gls{HSDS} via \texttt{h5pyd}, Zarr via \texttt{zarr}+ \texttt{s3fs}, Parquet via \texttt{pyarrow}, TileDB via \texttt{tiledb-py}, and ROOT/RNTuple via \texttt{uproot}/ROOT.

\subsection{Data Ingestion and Configuration}
A single detector dataset of \(\sim\)\SI{20}{\gibi\byte} (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames) was used. It was derived from 80 real experiment frames and expanded synthetically to the target size using a jitter-and-noise procedure. The first phase computes the number of additional frames needed to reach the target size, then generates the remaining frames in parallel and writes them as CBF. For each storage format, three datasets were created—one for each codec (gzip, LZ4, and zstd).

\subsubsection{Per-format data layout}
The logical array shape was (\text{frame},y,x) with (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames at 16-bit depth. \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS} and Zarr used frame-major chunking (one frame per chunk). TileDB uses tile extents (1, y, x) with row-major cell order. Parquet stores one row per frame with a binary payload column. ROOT/RNTuple stored one event per frame.

\subsection{Experimental Parameters and Workloads}
The primary aim of the experiment is to compare the performance of the five storage formats under two different access patterns: small-slice reads and full-dataset sequential scans. These access patterns reflect typical usage scenarios in beamline experiments, where scientists need quick access to specific data slices for real-time feedback or to process entire datasets for post-experiment analysis. Runs were executed in sessions, one cell (format$\times$codec$\times$pattern) at a time. Within-session run order was randomized with a fixed seed.

\subsubsection{Small-Slice Reads}
In a small-slice access pattern, data retrieval is performed on random single-frame reads; each frame averages \(\sim\)\SI{15.82}{\mebi\byte}. No tabular dataset was benchmarked. This pattern simulates the real-time, interactive data retrieval during and after experiments.

\subsubsection{Full-Dataset Sequential Scans}
In this pattern, a sequential read of the entire \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} dataset for each format–codec combination. This pattern stresses the storage system's bandwidth capacity, requiring high throughput to process large volumes of data efficiently.

\subsection{Performance Metrics and Evaluation Criteria}
We evaluate each format with:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Latency (slice and full):} GM time with dependence-robust 95\% CIs (log-scale analysis via MBB) and $p_{95}$ with MBB CIs.
  \item \textbf{Throughput (full scans):} GM GiB/s derived from bytes/time, analyzed on the log scale with MBB 95\% CIs.
  \item \textbf{Request intensity:} GETs per repetition and GETs per GiB from CloudWatch \emph{session} aggregates (descriptive only; no CIs).
  \item \textbf{Compression:} total stored bytes on S3 and compression ratio relative to the raw baseline.
\end{itemize}

Cross-format contrasts are reported as \emph{descriptive} GM time ratios within each codec$\times$pattern family.

\subsection{Decision-matrix scoring (normalization and weights)}
For downstream selection, we compute normalized 0–100 scores per codec\(\times\)pattern using robust min–max scaling within that family (lower bound = 5th percentile, upper bound = 95th percentile, clipped).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slice weights:} GM latency 0.60 (lower better), \(p_{95}\) 0.20 (lower), GETs/GiB 0.10 (lower), stored GiB 0.10 (lower).
    \item \textbf{Full-scan weights:} throughput 0.60 (higher), GETs/GiB 0.15 (lower), stored GiB 0.15 (lower), \(p_{95}\) 0.10 (lower).
\end{itemize}

League tables rare rankedby the primary objective (slice: GM latency; full: throughput) ,with tie-breakers matching the Results section.



\subsection{Statistical methods}
Timings are positive and multiplicative; we therefore analyze \(\ln(\text{time})\) per cell (format\(\times\)codec\(\times\)pattern). 

Primary estimands are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Geometric-mean (GM) time} with 95\% CIs via a moving-block bootstrap (MBB) on \(\ln(\text{time})\), baseline block lengths \(\ell\in\{4,6,8\}\), resamples \(B{=}10{,}000\), fixed seed. CIs are back-transformed where we report the widest CI across \(\ell\). Robustness extends to \(\ell=10\) (reported in sensitivity checks).
  \item \textbf{Tail latency (p95)} using the sample quantile (Type 7) with MBB 95\% CIs on the original scale where sensitivity recomputes $p_{95}$ with the Harrell–Davis estimator and \(\ell\in\{4,6,8,10\}\).
  \item \textbf{Full-scan throughput} (GiB/s) analyzed on the log scale from bytes/time with the same MBB settings.
\end{itemize}

Within each codec\(\times\)pattern, cross-format contrasts are presented as GM time \emph{ratios} versus a reference (Zarr) and are labeled \textbf{descriptive, session-conditional} (formats ran in separate sessions and no cross-session inference). Diagnostics per cell include Q–Q plots on \(\ln(\text{time})\), lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho_1\), and a run-order trend test (Spearman).

CloudWatch metrics (\texttt{GetRequestsSum}, \texttt{BytesDownloadedSum}, \texttt{FirstByteLatency\{Avg,p95\}}, \texttt{TotalRequestLatency\{Avg,p95\}}) are recorded per \emph{session} and used descriptively only. We do not average percentiles across sessions or attach inferential CIs. Warm-ups are excluded by design; \(n{=}50\) timed runs per cell are analyzed.


\subsection{Uncertainty and confounders in AWS}
AWS introduces time-varying latency and variance that cannot be fully controlled. We mitigated this issue by using a single region with the S3 bucket co-located, a Gateway VPC endpoint, fixed client settings, five warm-ups per session, and a randomized within-session order (with a fixed seed). Formats were executed in \emph{separate sessions} with no cross-format overlap. Consequently, cross-format contrasts are \textbf{descriptive, session-conditional}. Inference is limited to within-cell estimands (GM, p95, throughput) with dependence-robust bootstrap CIs. CloudWatch aggregates are per session and are reported descriptively (no inference, no cross-session averaging of percentiles).

\subsubsection*{Deviation: Parquet pilot-only}
Parquet was piloted but excluded from the timed results due to its single-object layout, which had a large metadata open time and object growth. The pilot and rationale are documented in Chapter \ref{ch:whatYouDid}, where Parquet remains as a qualitative discussion only. Timed comparisons cover four formats: \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple}.


\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:dataCollection}
The data‐collection plan for this thesis centers on a single dataset built from 80 real seed frames and synthetically expanded to \(\sim\)\SI{20}{\gibi\byte} (\(\sim\)1{,}300 frames). These frames statistically replicate the size, structure, and access patterns of real synchrotron and neutron experiments. Generating the datasets on demand based on a set of a few real frames (rather than distributing a whole raw beamline dataset) avoids licensing constraints and guarantees that every experiment can be reproduced bit-for-bit.

\subsection{Data Sources}
Detector stack ($\sim$\SI{20}{\gibi\byte}): $\sim$1{,}300 CBF frames at 16-bit depth. Eighty real frames from one experiment serve as seeds. A jitter–noise routine perturbs intensity, offset, spatial shifts, and noise to synthesize the remaining frames. Generation runs in two phases: (i) compute how many frames are required to reach the target size; (ii) synthesize them in parallel and write CBF files. Seed paths and bit depth are parameterized for reproducibility.


\subsection{Sampling Strategy}
Two patterns were executed on the same dataset. For the slice workload, each run reads one randomly selected frame (\(\sim\)\SI{15.82}{\mebi\byte}); no cross-format chunk-alignment was enforced. For the scanning workload, the entire \SI{20}{\gibi\byte} dataset is read sequentially. Within-session run order was randomized with a fixed seed, and no cross-format concurrency occurred.


\subsection{Instrumentation and Metric Logging}
Full-scan time is measured from the first GET dispatch to the last byte received, and slice-read time is per selected frame. Throughput equals bytes divided by time. We therefore collect the following data:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Client} start/stop timestamps and bytes per run.
  \item \textbf{CloudWatch S3} per-session aggregates: \texttt{GetRequestsSum}, \texttt{BytesDownloadedSum}, \texttt{FirstByteLatency\{Avg,p95\}}, \texttt{TotalRequestLatency\{Avg,p95\}} (descriptive only; no cross-session averaging of percentiles).
  \item \textbf{S3 sizes} per format–codec for compression calculations.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Validity and Reliability Measures}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Warm-up runs}: The first five iterations are discarded.
  \item \textbf{Replication}: every \{\emph{format, codec, pattern}\} tuple is executed with 5 warm-ups then $n=50$ timed runs.
\end{itemize}

No explicit OS cache flushes were performed between runs. Five warm-ups amortize cold-start effects (DNS, TLS, TCP, metadata caches).


\subsection{Reproducibility and availability}
Random seeds are fixed and logged with per-run timers, and CloudWatch aggregates are saved as JSON with run IDs. The code and notebooks will be released with a small public data subset sufficient to reproduce representative results via GitHub. Object paths, formats, and codec settings are documented to enable independent replication.

\section{Dataset and preprocessing}
This section specifies the dataset used in all experiments and the exact preprocessing applied. It states the raw frame geometry and encoding, then documents how we scaled from 80 real \texttt{CBF} frames to a $\sim$20\,GiB stack (\,$\sim$1{,}300 frames\,).

\subsection{Raw structure}
Each detector exposure is a dense two-dimensional frame. The specifications below fix the frame geometry, axis conventions, numeric encoding, byte size, and the theoretical versus empirically observed value range used for all subsequent ingestion and synthetic data generation.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Array shape per frame: $(2880, 2880)$.
  \item Axes: slow = rows, fast = columns (units: pixels).
  \item \texttt{dtype}: \texttt{uint16} (bit depth 16).
  \item Endianness: native little-endian.
  \item Per-frame size: \SI{16588800}{\byte} (\SI{15.82}{\mebi\byte}).
  \item Value range (theoretical): $[0, 65535]$.
  \item Value range (observed, raw): $[0, 1199]$.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Preprocessing and synthetic augmentation}
We started from $80$ raw \texttt{CBF} detector frames. Frames were enumerated in zero-based order, and recorded in a manifest that fixes the frame-index \,$\rightarrow$\, key mapping used later during ingestion. No resampling, normalization, or pixel edits were applied before augmentation.

\subsubsection{Augmentation method}
To scale the corpus for I/O benchmarking while preserving frame geometry and \texttt{uint16} encoding, additional frames were synthesized from the real frames using simple, distribution-preserving perturbations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Intensity gain: multiply by $g\in[0.85,1.15]$.
  \item Local contrast scale around the frame mean: factor $c\in[0.9,1.1]$.
  \item Spatial jitter: with probability $0.8$, apply integer circular shifts up to $\pm16$\,px on each axis.
  \item Additive noise: with probability $0.9$, add zero-mean Gaussian noise with $\sigma\approx 20$ counts.
  \item Clip to $[0,65535]$ and cast back to \texttt{uint16}.
\end{itemize}

Random choices use Python’s \texttt{random} and NumPy PRNGs. The experiment and data generation were executed with the fixed seed \texttt{979969114}.

\subsubsection{Size targeting and parallelization}
We targeted a detector stack of $\sim$\SI{20}{\gibi\byte}. The average on-disk slice size was estimated from existing outputs (or a one-slice probe), then the remaining slice count was computed to reach the target. Outputs were written as \texttt{slice\_\{index\}.cbf} files in a directory and then uploaded to \gls{AWS} \gls{SThree} ingestion pipeline.

\section{Storage layouts, codecs, and objectization}
This section defines how the array was mapped to \gls{SThree} for each format and how the notebook performed the writes.

\subsection{Common design and parity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Logical array for all stores: $(N,2880,2880)$, $N\approx 1300$, \texttt{uint16}.
  \item One frame per chunk/tile: $(1,2880,2880)$ to keep read units comparable.
  \item Codecs targeted: gzip, lz4, zstd. Parquet used gzip and lz4 only.
  \item Frame enumeration: zero-based; source files named \texttt{slice\_\{index\}.cbf}.
  \item Concurrency: threaded frame loading; batch writes by contiguous index slices.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{HDF5 via HSDS}
\gls{HSDS} serves \gls{HDFfive} on the EC2 node; we record domain paths, dataset geometry, chunking and codec settings. \gls{HSDS} manages \gls{SThree} keys and metadata; one frame per chunk preserves parity with other stores.


\subsubsection{Layout}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \gls{HSDS} domains: \texttt{/hdf5/\{gzip|lz4|zstd\}/all\_data.h5}.
  \item Dataset: \texttt{/data}, shape $(N,2880,2880)$, chunks $(1,2880,2880)$, \texttt{uint16}.
  \item Compression: \texttt{compression=\{gzip|lz4|zstd\}} (library default levels).
  \item Objectization: $\sim N$ chunk objects plus \gls{HSDS} metadata/index objects.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Ingestion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Writer: \texttt{h5pyd} client to \gls{HSDS} endpoint.
  \item Batching: frames loaded in batches of $64$; assigned as \texttt{dset[start:end,:,:] = stack}.
  \item Concurrency: \texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor} up to $32$ workers for frame decode/IO.
  \item Notes: \gls{HSDS} manages \gls{SThree} keys; no client-side chunk cache tuning recorded.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Zarr on S3}
Zarr uses one chunk per frame; here we record store paths, array geometry, chunking parity, codecs, and the write procedure.

\subsubsection{Layout}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stores: \texttt{s3://scatterin-thesis/zarr/\{gzip|lz4|zstd\}.zarr}.
  \item Array: \texttt{/data}, shape $(N,2880,2880)$, chunks $(1,2880,2880)$, \texttt{uint16}.
  \item Compression: gzip \texttt{level=3}; zstd \texttt{level=3}; lz4 via Blosc \texttt{clevel=3}.
  \item Metadata: default Zarr metadata files; no consolidation step applied.
  \item Objectization: $\sim N$ chunk objects plus store metadata files.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Ingestion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Writer: \texttt{zarr} with \texttt{FsspecStore} using \gls{SThree} credentials.
  \item Batching: $64$ frames per batch; contiguous slice writes.
  \item Concurrency: threaded frame loading; assignment to array per batch.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Apache Parquet on S3}
Frames were tabularized as rows with 2D rows as fixed-size lists. \textit{Note:} Parquet was piloted only and is excluded from timed results due to an operational stop (see section \ref{subsubsec:parquet-pilot-exclusion}).

\subsubsection{Layout}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Files: \texttt{s3://scatterin-thesis/parquet/\{gzip|lz4\}.parquet}.
  \item Schema: $2880$ columns \texttt{row\_0}\dots\texttt{row\_2879}, each \texttt{list<fixed\_size=2880, uint16>}.
  \item Row groups: one appended row per frame (writer defaults).
  \item Compression: \texttt{GZIP} or \texttt{LZ4}; dictionaries disabled.

\subsubsection{Ingestion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Writer: \texttt{pyarrow.parquet.ParquetWriter} on \gls{SThree}.
  \item Loop: for each frame, build per-row arrays, assemble one-row table, write.

\subsubsection{Parquet pilot and exclusion (deviation)}
\label{subsubsec:parquet-pilot-exclusion}

Parquet was implemented as a single file with $2880$ list-of-$2880$ \texttt{uint16} columns (\texttt{row\_0}\dots\texttt{row\_2879}), one appended row per frame, with \texttt{GZIP} and \texttt{LZ4} compression; dictionaries disabled.

\paragraph{Observed during pilot}
\begin{itemize}
  \item File size: a single Parquet object of $\sim$\SI{34}{\gibi\byte} for a $\sim$\SI{20}{\gibi\byte} raw stack.
  \item Metadata open: \SIrange{3}{4}{\minute} to load schema, footer, and column indexes on the EC2 test node. The EC2 instance ended up crashing and hanging three times while trying to load GZIP parquet file.
  \item Read smoke test: one frame decoded after metadata load; further timing runs were halted.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Decision}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Operational stop after writing two frames and one read. Rationale: metadata load time and object size would make the full benchmark impractical within the experiment budget.
  \item Consequence: Parquet is omitted from timed latency/throughput results; artifacts are retained for transparency and discussed qualitatively in chapter \ref{ch:discussion}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{TileDB dense array}
TileDB stores a dense array with frame-sized tiles and immutable fragments; this records the domain, filters, and fragment layout.

\subsubsection{Domain and tiles}
\begin{itemize}
  \item URIs: \texttt{s3://scatterin-thesis/tiledb/\{gzip|lz4|zstd\}.tdb}.
  \item Domain dims: \texttt{file: [0,N-1]}, \texttt{y: [0,2879]}, \texttt{x: [0,2879]}.
  \item Tile extents: $(1,2880,2880)$ (full-frame tiles).
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Filters}
\begin{itemize}
  \item gzip (\texttt{level=3}), lz4 (\texttt{level=3}), zstd (\texttt{level=3}) as attribute filter lists.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Fragments and objectization}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Writes executed in batches of $64$ frames per task; each task produced a fragment.
  \item Expected fragments: $\lceil N/64\rceil$ (no post-write consolidation or vacuum).
  \item Each fragment contributes internal files (data, metadata, footers) under the array URI.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ROOT / RNTuple}
ROOT encodes all frames into a single file per codec; this notes the tree layout, compression, and access pattern.

\subsubsection{Tree layout}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Files: \texttt{s3://scatterin-thesis/root/\{gzip|lz4|zstd\}.root}.
  \item \texttt{TTree} \texttt{img\_tree} with one branch \texttt{data} of length $2880\times2880$ \texttt{uint16}; one entry per frame.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Compression}
\begin{itemize}
  \item gzip: algorithm \texttt{kZLIB}, level $3$; lz4: \texttt{kLZ4} (default level); zstd: \texttt{kZSTD} (default level).
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Objectization}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Single ROOT object per codec. Selective reads use HTTP/S3 range-GETs into baskets/pages.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Read mapping (planned behavior, qualitative)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Frame-sized slice: \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB $\Rightarrow$ one chunk/tile read per frame $+$ small metadata; Parquet and ROOT $\Rightarrow$ range reads within a single object.
  \item Full scan: \gls{HDFfive}/\gls{HSDS} and Zarr $\Rightarrow$ $\sim N$ chunk reads; TileDB $\Rightarrow$ sequential tile reads across fragments; Parquet and ROOT $\Rightarrow$ sequential range reads within one object.
\end{itemize}


\section{Execution protocol}
All reads ran on a single EC2 node in \texttt{eu-north-1}. For each \emph{format}~$\times$~\emph{codec}~$\times$~\emph{pattern}, the notebook executed a fixed warm-up. There is a fixed time gap between the real execution and warm-ups to keep the metrics out of \gls{SThree}. No cross-format concurrency.

\subsection{Sessions and schedule}
Each \emph{session} targeted one cell (format, codec, pattern). Order within a session:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Warm-up reads: $5$ runs, not recorded.
  \item Idle gap: \(\ge\)2\,minutes to avoid mixing warm-ups into CloudWatch’s 1-minute aggregation.
  \item Timed runs: $50$ repetitions, recorded.
\end{enumerate}

Between sessions, formats did not overlap. HSDS, Zarr, TileDB, and ROOT were run in separate sessions.

\subsection{Randomization and seeding}
A fixed seed ensured reproducibility (\texttt{SEED = 979969114}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item Slice pattern indices: function \texttt{\_idxs(n)} returns \(5+50\) frame indices. If \(n\ge 55\): uniform without replacement; else: with replacement.
  \item Full scans, scans all the files in the dataset, so no index list is being used.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Timing definition}
Per run, we measured wall-clock time using \texttt{time.perf\_counter\_ns}. The timer encloses a single read call. It includes network transfer, decompression, and client decode. Log serialization is outside the timer.

\subsection{Pattern procedures}
\subsubsection{Full scan}

Exactly one complete pass over all frames per repetition.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Zarr: \texttt{data[:, :, :]}.
  \item TileDB: \texttt{data[:, :, :]}.
  \item HDF5/HSDS: \texttt{data[:, :, :]}.
  \item ROOT/RNTuple: for \(i=0\dots n-1\) call \texttt{br.array(entry\_start=i, entry\_stop=i+1, library="np")}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Slice (single-frame) reads}
Exactly one frame per repetition, using the precomputed index list.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Zarr: \texttt{data[idx, :, :]}.
  \item TileDB: \texttt{data[idx, :, :]}.
  \item HDF5/HSDS: \texttt{data[idx, :, :]}.
  \item ROOT/RNTuple: \texttt{br.array(entry\_start=idx, entry\_stop=idx+1, library="np")}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{CloudWatch capture}
\gls{SThree} bucket request metrics were enabled with the dimensions \{\texttt{BucketName= scatterin-thesis}, \texttt{FilterId= root}\}. For each session, we queried and stored:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{GetRequests Sum}, \texttt{BytesDownloaded Sum}
  \item \texttt{FirstByteLatency Average} and \texttt{p95}
  \item \texttt{TotalRequestLatency Average} and \texttt{p95}
\end{itemize}

Warm-ups precede the timed window by \(\ge\)2\, minutes to keep aggregates attributable to the timed runs.

\subsection{Inclusion, failures, and retries}
Warm-ups are excluded by design. A timed run is included if the read returns successfully. If a read raised an exception or timed out, that repetition was re-attempted.å

\subsection{Caching and connections}
Default client behavior was used. No explicit OS page-cache flushes. HTTP keep-alive and connection pooling are left to \texttt{fsspec/s3fs}, \texttt{boto3}, \texttt{h5pyd}, \texttt{tiledb} VFS, and \texttt{uproot}. No application-level prefetching was enabled in the read loops.


\subsection{Run IDs, filenames, and logs}
Each session emits a monotonic \texttt{run\_id} (UTC \texttt{YYYYMMDD-HHMMSS}). Timed samples are written as JSON:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Zarr: \texttt{logs/zarr\_\{codec\}\_\{pattern\}\_\{run\_id\}.json}
  \item TileDB: \texttt{logs/tiledb\_\{codec\}\_\{pattern\}\_\{run\_id\}.json}
  \item HDF5/HSDS: \texttt{logs/hdf5\_\{codec\}\_\{pattern\}\_\{run\_id\}.json}
  \item ROOT: \texttt{logs/root\_\{codec\}\_\{pattern\}\_\{run\_id\}.json}
\end{itemize}

Each file contains JSON rows of the form:
\begin{verbatim}
[ {"i": <rep>, "t_total_ns": <int>}, ... ]
\end{verbatim}

CloudWatch aggregates for the same cell are saved as
\begin{verbatim}
logs/<format>_<codec>_<pattern>_cloudwatch.json
\end{verbatim}





\section{Quality control, exclusions, and deviations}
Runs followed a fixed warm-up and timing protocol. Integrity checks verified shapes and dtypes. Only successful timed reads were included.

\subsection{Quality control}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inclusion: $5$ warm-ups excluded; $50$ timed repetitions included per cell.
  \item Isolation for \gls{SThree} metrics: warm-ups \(\rightarrow\) idle gap \(\ge\)2\,min \(\rightarrow\) timed runs.
  \item Integrity checks: spot decode of returned arrays; shape \(=(2880,2880)\), dtype \texttt{uint16}.
  \item CloudWatch capture: session-scoped queries saved as JSON; notebook timers saved per run.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Exclusion rules}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Exclude if: timeout, HTTP 5xx, partial read, decode error.
  \item Handling: Abort, and re-run the entire execution test.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Deviations from plan}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Parquet pilot-only} Writing and a smoke read revealed impractical metadata open times and a single huge object; Parquet was omitted from timed results and kept for qualitative discussion.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Results and Analysis}
\label{ch:resultsAndAnalysis}
This chapter reports the empirical results of benchmarking four storage backends on Amazon \gls{SThree}, \gls{HDFfive} via \gls{HSDS}, Zarr, TileDB, and \gls{ROOT}/\gls{RNTuple} under two workload patterns on a single $\sim$20 GiB detector stack ($\sim$1{,}300 frames, \texttt{uint16}). Parquet is included as a pilot-only storage engine (no timed results). Each backend is evaluated with three codecs (gzip, LZ4, zstd). We present outcomes for (i) partial retrieval of a single frame ($\sim$15.82 MiB), and (ii) full sequential scans of the dataset.

Metrics encompass client-observed timing, service-side behavior, and storage footprint, with timings analyzed on a log scale. For each {format$\times$codec$\times$pattern} cell, we report geometric mean (GM) time with dependence-robust 95\% CIs via a moving-block bootstrap (MBB; $\ell\in\{4,6,8\}$; $B{=}10{,}000$). The tail behavior is reported via $p_{95}$ with the same bootstrap. For full scans, we also report throughput (GiB/s) derived from bytes/time and analyzed on the log scale with the same bootstrap. The storage footprint is reported as the number of stored bytes and the compression ratio relative to the raw storage. Request intensity (GETs/rep, GETs/GiB) is derived from CloudWatch session aggregates of all runs within a cell.

Cross-format contrasts are \textbf{descriptive, session-conditional} because formats run in separate sessions. Warm-ups are excluded; $n=50$ timed repetitions per cell are analyzed. Exploratory nonparametric summaries and tail-sensitivity checks are provided.

\subsection{S3 object sizes and compression ratios}
 \label{subsec:comp_ratio}
This section enumerates the total stored bytes on \gls{SThree} for each {format, codec} and reports compression ratios relative to the uncompressed array baseline. Stored bytes are computed by summing the sizes of all objects under the format–codec prefix (data and metadata files included). \section{Request intensity and S3 service latencies}
This section summarizes S3 request behavior and service-side latencies to provide context for client timings. We report the number of GETs per session, per repetition, and per GiB. CloudWatch FirstByte/TotalRequest averages and $p_{95}$ are considered descriptive because they are aggregated, session-conditional metrics. Because these are session aggregates, we report \emph{point estimates only}.

\subsection{GETs per repetition and per GiB}
We summarize request intensity from CloudWatch \emph{session} aggregates. All $n$ timed runs in a cell (format$\times$codec$\times$pattern) contribute to one set of counts. Let $G$ be \texttt{GetRequestsSum} and $B$ be \texttt{BytesDownloadedSum}. Counts include S3 \texttt{GET} only; \texttt{HEAD}/\texttt{LIST}/\texttt{PUT} are excluded. Warm-ups are excluded and separated temporally from the timed window.

Per-repetition intensity:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gets_per_rep}
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{rep}}=\frac{G}{n}.
\end{equation}

Volume-normalized intensity:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gets_per_gib}
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{GiB}}=\frac{G\,2^{30}}{B}.
\end{equation}

Here $n$ is the number of timed repetitions in the cell; $\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{GiB}}$ is measured in GETs per GiB.

Rates are descriptive and session-conditional. Table \ref{tab:req_intensity} lists $\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{rep}}$ and $\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{GiB}}$ for all cells. \chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}
This chapter interprets the results, links them to layout and request behavior, and states the limits. Note that all cross‑format contrasts remain descriptive and session‑conditional.

\section{Bottom-line outcomes}
\label{sec:discussion-bottomline}
This section reports winners by workload and codec, using GM latency (slices) and GM throughput (full scans) with dependence-robust 95\% CIs and $p_{95}$ checks tail risk. All contrasts are \emph{descriptive} and \emph{session-conditional}.

\subsection{Slices: winners by codec}
Single‑frame reads have two stress areas: (i) layout/objectization, which sets how many objects and round–trips each request touches, and (ii) decode cost, which depends on the codec and the library path. We judge the winners by geometric mean (GM) latency with dependence robust 95\% CIs and use $p_{95}$ to flag tail risk. All cross–format contrasts are \emph{descriptive} and \emph{session–conditional}. References below point back to the Slice tables and figures for each codec.

\textbf{Zarr is the clear winner} for zstd slices. Its GM CI lies fully below TileDB’s, which is second, followed by HDF5/HSDS and then ROOT. The $p_{95}$ ordering mirrors the center, indicating Zarr combines a fast typical latency with controlled tails. Mechanistically, this matches a chunk–per–frame layout that amortizes S3 RTT via client concurrency, while zstd keeps the decode cost moderate. See Table~\ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:slice_zstd_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_zstd_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_zstd_ratios_point}. GM delta show that Zarr is \approx12\% faster than TileDB (Table \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}).

\textbf{Zarr remains fastest} on GM under lz4. \textbf{TileDB and HDF5/HSDS are close} behind it, with overlapping CIs between those two, where ROOT is slowest. A notable trade–off appears in the tails: Zarr’s $p_{95}$ is higher and less tight than TileDB and HDF5/HSDS, consistent with lz4’s bias toward decode speed over compactness. If the application is highly tail–sensitive, TileDB’s steadier $p_{95}$ may offset its slightly higher GM. See Table \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:slice_lz4_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_lz4_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_lz4_ratios_point}. GM deltas show that Zarr is \approx22\% faster than TileDB and \approx27\% faster than HDF5 via HSDS (Table \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb}).

\textbf{TileDB wins} gzip slices with non-overlapping GM CIs versus Zarr. Zarr is next and outperforms HDF5/HSDS, where ROOT again is slowest by a wide margin. This pattern aligns with gzip’s higher CPU cost, which leads to TileDB’s fewer, larger fragments reducing request overhead enough to compensate for gzip decode, while Zarr’s per–frame objects incur more round–trips. Ordering is consistent in $p_{95}$ and in the Zarr–referenced ratio view. See Table \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:slice_gzip_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_gzip_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:slice_gzip_ratios_point}. GM delta show that TileDB is \approx17\% faster than Zarr (Table \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}).

Across codecs, the winner patterns above align with request intensity evidence for slices: lower GETs/GiB often coincide with lower GM latency for Zarr and TileDB. At the same time, ROOT’s single–object layout shows that “few GETs” does not guarantee speed due to paging and decode path overhead. See Fig. \ref{fig:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets} and Table~\ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}.

Nonparametric confirmations reproduce the same slice rankings by codec, and Harrell–Davis $p_{95}$ with an $\ell$–grid preserves ordering, with the lz4/Zarr heavier–tail observation intact. See Fig. \ref{fig:np_slice_ratios_grid}; Tables \ref{tab:np_slice_gzip_median}, \ref{tab:np_slice_lz4_median}, \ref{tab:np_slice_zstd_median}, and the tail–sensitivity artifacts in Fig. \ref{fig:tail-slice-p95-hd-ci-vs-L} and Tables \ref{tab:tail-slice-gzip-p95-selected}–\ref{tab:tail-slice-zstd-p95-selected}.


\subsection{Full scans: throughput ordering}
Full–dataset reads test sustained sequential delivery and decode under continuous concurrency. We judge winners by geometric mean (GM) \emph{throughput} (GiB/s) with dependence–robust 95\% CIs derived from GM time on the log scale, where $p_{95}$ times are used to check stability. All cross–format contrasts are \emph{descriptive} and \emph{session–conditional}. The key result is simple: across \texttt{zstd}, \texttt{lz4}, and \texttt{gzip}, \textbf{TileDB leads by a wide margin}, \textbf{Zarr is consistently second}, and \textbf{HDF5/HSDS and ROOT come last}.

Effect sizes vs Zarr show that TileDB is $\approx$3.16$\times$ faster under \texttt{gzip} (11{,}254 ms vs 35{,}598 ms), $\approx$2.17$\times$ under \texttt{lz4} (13{,}602 ms vs 29{,}483 ms), and $\approx$2.92$\times$ under \texttt{zstd} (10{,}384 ms vs 30{,}365 ms); Table \ref{tab:full_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb}.

\textbf{TileDB is the clear throughput leader.} Zarr is a distant second, and HDF5/HSDS and ROOT are far behind. GM time and its CI show clean separation, and the ratio view reinforces TileDB’s advantage. $p_{95}$ times follow the same ordering, indicating stable long–scan behavior. See Table \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:full_zstd_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_zstd_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_zstd_ratios_point}.

\textbf{TileDB remains first and Zarr second.} Faster decode narrows absolute times but does not change ranks. HDF5/HSDS and ROOT stay well behind. $p_{95}$ times are tight for TileDB and Zarr, consistent with sustained data streaming. See Table \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:full_lz4_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_lz4_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_lz4_ratios_point}.

\textbf{TileDB still leads and Zarr is second.} Gzip’s higher CPU cost lifts absolute times for all formats, but ordering is unchanged. HDF5/HSDS and ROOT remain the slowest. $p_{95}$ is consistent with the GM picture. See Table \ref{tab:full_gzip_mbb} and Figs. \ref{fig:full_gzip_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_gzip_p95_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_gzip_ratios_point}.

Throughput aligns with request intensity and layout: TileDB attains high throughput with \emph{moderate} GETs/GiB due to fragment–backed long reads. Zarr is \emph{higher} on GETs/GiB yet parallelizes effectively, while HDF5 via HSDS shows \emph{higher} GETs/GiB with poorer streaming. ROOT has \emph{few} GETs/GiB but still has low throughput because a single–object layout, plus paging/decode, dominates. See Fig. \ref{fig:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets} and Table \ref{tab:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets}.

CloudWatch FirstByte and TotalRequest latencies are stable by session and do not explain cross–format gaps; the driver is layout and decode rather than “faster S3.” See Figs. \ref{fig:cw_firstbyte_grid}, \ref{fig:cw_total_grid}.

Nonparametric centers reproduce the same ranks across codecs, and the HD $p_{95}$ sensitivity keeps the ordering intact. See Fig. \ref{fig:np_full_ratios_grid} and Tables \ref{tab:np_full_gzip_median}, \ref{tab:np_full_lz4_median}, \ref{tab:np_full_zstd_median}, see also Fig. \ref{fig:tail-full-p95-hd-ci-vs-L} and Tables \ref{tab:tail-full-gzip-p95-selected}–\ref{tab:tail-full-zstd-p95-selected}.


\section{Mechanisms explaining outcomes}
This section explains \emph{why} the observed orderings occur. We link layout-driven objectization and request intensity (GETs/GiB) to client timings, show that S3 service latencies are stable and not causal, and summarize codec CPU–bytes trade-offs.

\subsection{Objectization and layout}
Objectization for each storage backend determines the request pattern. \textbf{Zarr/HSDS} materializes roughly one data object per frame (plus metadata), \textbf{TileDB} writes a small number of immutable \emph{fragments} (each with internal files), and \textbf{ROOT/RNTuple} stores a \emph{single} large object. The consequence is straightforward: per–frame layouts expose more opportunities for client concurrency. Where fragment layouts trade fewer objects for long sequential reads, and lastly single–object layouts minimize GET count but constrain parallelism to range requests within one key. This matches the data in Table \ref{tab:object_counts} and the distribution in Fig. \ref{fig:object_counts}. Practically, concurrency benefits Zarr/TileDB, where ROOT often shows \emph{few GETs yet slow} performance because paging and decoding dominate once request speed is reached.

\subsection{Request intensity vs client timing}
Lower request intensity \textit{often} aligns with better client outcomes for chunked/fragmented stores. Fewer GETs/GiB tend to coincide with lower slice GM and higher full-scan throughput for \textbf{Zarr} and \textbf{TileDB}. See the slice correlation in Fig. \ref{fig:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets} and Table \ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}, and the scan view in Fig. \ref{fig:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets} and Table \ref{tab:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets}. The pattern is not universal, where \textbf{ROOT} is the counterexample. It has \emph{very} low GETs/GiB yet poor latency/throughput due to the single-object layout, plus small pages and decode costs limit effective parallelism. GETs/GiB explains part of the variance, while layout and decode paths account for the rest.


\subsection{Service latencies are not the driver}
CloudWatch \emph{FirstByteLatency} and \emph{TotalRequestLatency} are stable within sessions (both avg and $p_{95}$) and do not track the large cross–format gaps seen in client timings. The dominant factors are request pattern and decode path, not “faster S3.” Treat these metrics as context, not causal evidence. See Figs. \ref{fig:cw_firstbyte_grid}, \ref{fig:cw_total_grid}.


\subsection{Codec effects}
Codecs trade CPU for bytes. \texttt{lz4} minimizes decode time and helps latency where per–chunk CPU dominates. \texttt{zstd} strikes a balance between compression and decoding, while \texttt{gzip} yields the smallest stored size but is slower to decode. Stored size is primarily \emph{codec–driven} (typically \texttt{zstd}\,$\approx$\,\texttt{gzip}\,$<$\,\texttt{lz4}), but format deltas can be large under \texttt{lz4} which was shown in our data that HDF5 via HSDS stored 19.17\,GiB vs Zarr 10.40\,GiB and TileDB 15.56\,GiB (Table
 \ref{tab:object_counts} and see also Figs. \ref{fig:stored_bytes}, \ref{fig:compression_rates}). Treat \texttt{lz4}+HSDS as storage-heavy. These trade–offs align with the winners in Section \ref{sec:discussion-bottomline} where Zarr leads \texttt{zstd}/\texttt{lz4} slices while TileDB leads \texttt{gzip} slices (Tables \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}). TileDB dominates full-scan throughput across codecs (Tables \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_gzip_mbb}).


\section{Distribution shape and robustness}
This section verifies that the modeling choices are appropriate and yield similar results to those of other estimators. We first assess shape and serial dependence to justify log–time analysis using the moving–block bootstrap (MBB), then confirm rankings with median centers, and finally test tail stability with Harrell–Davis $p_{95}$.

\subsection{Shape and dependence}
Log–time is appropriate, and serial dependence is present. The diagnostics table shows frequent non-normality in the raw time series and improved behavior in $\ln(\text{time})$, while positive lag-1 autocorrelation is observed across all cells. This both motivates analyzing centers on the log scale and using MBB CIs that respect dependence (Table \ref{tab:timing_diag_main}). Q–Q grids on $\ln(\text{time})$ support the transformation, and run-order traces show no systematic drift beyond short-range dependence (Appendix Figs. \ref{fig:appendix_qq_grid}, \ref{fig:appendix_drift_grid}). Together, these justify GM on log–time with dependence-robust CIs.


\subsection{Center robustness}
Median-based rankings match GM across codecs and patterns. Descriptive median ratios reproduce the slice and full-scan orderings, and rank-agreement tables show identical ranks to GM within each codec (Figs. \ref{fig:np_slice_ratios_grid}, \ref{fig:np_full_ratios_grid} and Tables \ref{tab:np_slice_gzip_median}, \ref{tab:np_slice_lz4_median}, \ref{tab:np_slice_zstd_median}, \ref{tab:np_full_gzip_median}, \ref{tab:np_full_lz4_median}, \ref{tab:np_full_zstd_median}, and \ref{tab:np_slice_gzip_rank}, \ref{tab:np_slice_lz4_rank}, \ref{tab:np_slice_zstd_rank}, \ref{tab:np_full_gzip_rank}, \ref{tab:np_full_lz4_rank}, \ref{tab:np_full_zstd_rank}).


\subsection{Tail robustness}
Orderings are stable under Harrell–Davis $p_{95}$ with an $\ell\in\{4,6,8,10\}$ block-length grid, where we report the widest dependence-robust CI per cell. The notable exception is \texttt{lz4}/Zarr, which shows heavier tails relative to its peers, but this does not overturn the winner hierarchy established by GM and median centers (Figs. \ref{fig:tail-slice-p95-hd-ci-vs-L}, \ref{fig:tail-full-p95-hd-ci-vs-L} and Tables \ref{tab:tail-slice-gzip-p95-selected}, \ref{tab:tail-slice-lz4-p95-selected}, \ref{tab:tail-slice-zstd-p95-selected}, \ref{tab:tail-full-gzip-p95-selected}, \ref{tab:tail-full-lz4-p95-selected}, \ref{tab:tail-full-zstd-p95-selected}).



\section{Cost and storage posture}
Stored size is mostly codec‑driven, however, \texttt{lz4} showed significant format variation (HSDS 19.17\,GiB vs Zarr 10.40\,GiB vs TileDB 15.56\,GiB; Table \ref{tab:object_counts}). For compact storage, prefer \texttt{zstd} or \texttt{gzip} and avoid \texttt{lz4}+HSDS unless storage is unconstrained.

\subsection{Request cost proxy}
For slice-heavy workloads, watch \emph{GETs/GiB}. \textbf{TileDB} and \textbf{Zarr} typically deliver lower GETs/GiB at competitive or better latency than compared with \textbf{HDF5/HSDS}. This indicates a more favorable request-cost posture at equal performance. See Table \ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets} and Fig. \ref{fig:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}.

\subsection{Storage cost proxy}
Stored size is mainly \emph{codec-driven}, where \textbf{zstd} and \textbf{gzip} compress more than \textbf{lz4}. Between-format differences at a fixed codec are small and arise from layout metadata, rather than the payload. See Figs. \ref{fig:stored_bytes}, \ref{fig:compression_rates}.

\subsection{Counterexample}
\textbf{ROOT} shows that a few requests do not imply speed or efficiency. It has the lowest \emph{GETs/GiB} yet poor slice latency and scan throughput due to single-object paging and decode overhead. See Tables \ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}, \ref{tab:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets} and the per-codec MBB results (slices: Tables \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}; full scans: Tables \ref{tab:full_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb}).



\section{Validity and limits}
This section states what the measurements support and where they do not generalize.

\subsection{Internal checks}
All planned runs were completed without failure, and warm-ups were excluded by design. The order was randomized with a fixed seed. Serial dependence is present and handled via the moving-block bootstrap, and the log-time choice is supported by shape diagnostics. See the section \ref{sec:run-inclusion-and-coverage} and Table \ref{tab:timing_diag_main} for Shapiro $p$ and $\rho_1$.

\subsection{External validity}
Findings reflect one dataset, one region, and one client. We measure two access patterns with three different codecs. Cross-format contrasts are \emph{descriptive} and \emph{session-conditional}. CloudWatch service metrics are used as descriptive context only, not as causal evidence. See the Results section headers for scope and Figs. \ref{fig:cw_firstbyte_grid}, \ref{fig:cw_total_grid} for the service-latency context.

\section{Deviations and exclusions}
Parquet was piloted only and excluded from timed comparisons. Early exploration used small subsets and smoke tests, so the format was only lightly exercised. When we scaled to complete ingestion and attempted full-dataset scans, the issues surfaced: a \emph{single} large object (~34 GiB for ~20 GiB raw) incurred 3–4 min metadata open time and intermittent client instability on the GZIP file, making it unsuitable for frame-granularity reads and unattractive even for bulk scans in this setup. The root cause is objectization and file semantics: a single-object, columnar Parquet file offers few request-level parallelization opportunities and concentrates footer/metadata work at open. We therefore retain Parquet as a qualitative caution for n-D image stacks on S3 single-object layouts and exclude it from quantitative timing (Chapter \ref{ch:resultsAndAnalysis}). A partitioned Parquet \emph{dataset} with row-grouping aligned to frames might alter objectization, but designing and validating that variant was out of scope.

\chapter{Conclusions and Future work}
\label{ch:conclusionsAndFutureWork}
Zarr yields the lowest slice latency under \texttt{zstd} and \texttt{lz4}, where TileDB wins slices under \texttt{gzip}. TileDB dominates full‑scan throughput across codecs by 2.2–3.2$\times$ vs Zarr, with stable tails. ROOT remains slow for frame‑random reads despite a few GETs.

The mechanism for each backend technology is divided into its layout and request behavior. Per-frame objectization (Zarr, HDF5 via HSDS) and fragment layouts (TileDB) expose client concurrency, whereas a single-object layout (ROOT) minimizes GETs but constrains parallelism and incurs paging/decode overhead. Codec choice trades CPU for bytes where \texttt{zstd} and \texttt{gzip} are generally more compact than \texttt{lz4}.  In our data HDF5 via HSDS with \texttt{lz4} stored 19.17\, GiB versus Zarr 10.40\, GiB and TileDB 15.56\, GiB at the same payload size, indicating a storage-heavy configuration.

Winners are judged by geometric-mean (GM) latency for slices and GM throughput for full scans, with dependence-robust 95\% CIs via a moving-block bootstrap (block lengths $\ell\in\{4,6,8,10\}$; $B{=}10{,}000$) and Harrell–Davis $p_{95}$ for tails. Findings reflect one $\sim$20\, GiB dataset, one region (eu-north-1), and one client; CloudWatch service latencies are used as context, not as causal drivers.

The remainder of the chapter provides a closing synthesis, decision-oriented recommendations, operational implications per backend (metadata consolidation, fragment management, cache and paging settings), and a prioritized agenda for future work: multi-client scaling and caches, consolidation strategies, codec/filter and layout sweeps, broader datasets and access patterns, cross-platform cost surfaces, and decision-matrix v2.



\section{Closing synthesis}
Across single-frame slices and full scans, the ordering is consistent and session-conditional. For slices, \textbf{Zarr} is fastest under \texttt{zstd} and \texttt{lz4}, while \textbf{TileDB} wins under \texttt{gzip}. \textbf{HDF5/HSDS} comes not far behind but where \textbf{ROOT} is slowest. For full scans, \textbf{TileDB} delivers the highest throughput across all codecs by a wide margin, with \textbf{Zarr} second and \textbf{HDF5/HSDS} and \textbf{ROOT} far behind. The mechanism is objectization plus request intensity; the per-frame objects (Zarr/HSDS) and fragments (TileDB) enable client concurrency, while a single object (ROOT) minimizes GETs but limits parallelism and pays paging/decode overhead. This means that \emph{few GETs} does not imply speed; this linkage appears in the cross-metric views (\ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}, \ref{tab:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets}). Cost properties follow: request cost tracks GETs/GiB (important for slice-heavy use), while stored GiB is codec-driven (zstd$\approx$gzip $<$ lz4). Therefore, choose \texttt{zstd} or \texttt{gzip} when space dominates and \texttt{lz4} when CPU/latency dominates. If one backend must serve both interactive slices and bulk scans, start with \emph{Zarr+\texttt{zstd}} for UI latency and accept lower scan throughput. However, if scans dominate, choose \emph{TileDB+\texttt{zstd}} and keep frame‑aligned tiles.




\section{Practitioner choices (decision matrix hook)}
This section turns the empirical rankings into concrete decisions. Choices are related to the primary objective of each workload. All evidence is descriptive and session–conditional, and ties back to the league tables and per–codec slice/full results.



\subsection{Interactive slices (latency–first)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Default:} \emph{Zarr + zstd}. It delivers the lowest slice GM with controlled tails across our setting, and scales with client concurrency (Tables~\ref{tab:league-slice-zstd}, \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}).
    \item \textbf{If gzip is mandated:} \emph{TileDB + gzip}. TileDB’s fragment layout offsets gzip’s CPU cost and wins on GM with non-overlapping CIs versus Zarr (Tables \ref{tab:league-slice-gzip}, \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}).
    \item \textbf{Caution:} Avoid \emph{HSDS+\texttt{lz4}} if storage cost matters because it stored 19.17\,GiB vs Zarr 10.40\,GiB at the same codec (Table \ref{tab:object_counts}).
    \item \textbf{Avoid for frame-random reads:} \emph{ROOT}. Few GETs do not translate to low latency due to paging and decode (Tables \ref{tab:league-slice-gzip}, \ref{tab:league-slice-lz4}, \ref{tab:league-slice-zstd} and Tables \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}).
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Bulk scans (throughput–first)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Default:} \emph{TileDB + zstd or lz4}. TileDB dominates full-scan throughput across codecs. Choose \texttt{lz4} if CPU is tight, \texttt{zstd} if space matters (Tables \ref{tab:league-full-zstd}, \ref{tab:league-full-lz4} and Tables \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb}).
    \item \textbf{Second choice when ecosystem fit matters:} \emph{Zarr} (any of zstd/lz4/gzip) trades some throughput for broader tooling while retaining stable streaming of data (Tables \ref{tab:league-full-gzip}, \ref{tab:league-full-lz4}, \ref{tab:league-full-zstd} and Tables \ref{tab:full_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:full_zstd_mbb}).  
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Weight sensitivity}
Top selections remain stable under the stated weights, as the selected weights do not alter the winners in our data. Where near-ties appear within a codec×pattern bucket, we call them out in the league tables and recommend using the tie-breakers (slice: $p_{95}\!\rightarrow$ GETs/GiB $\rightarrow$ stored GiB and for full scan GM time $\!\rightarrow$ GETs/GiB $\rightarrow$ stored GiB). See the score tables (\ref{tab:scores-slice-gzip}, \ref{tab:scores-slice-lz4}, \ref{tab:scores-slice-zstd}, \ref{tab:scores-full-gzip}, \ref{tab:scores-full-lz4}, \ref{tab:scores-full-zstd}).




\section{Operational implications}
This section converts the Results into backend-specific operational actions. Recommendations cover metadata consolidation, prefix sharding, chunk/tile sizing, fragment consolidation, cache tuning, and read-page batching. Measured object counts justify each item, GETs/GiB, and $p_{95}$ tails.

\subsection{Zarr}
\textbf{Consolidate metadata} to cut LIST/HEAD fan‑out and reduce cold‑open overhead, because unconsolidated layouts inflate object count and amplify per‑request latency (Fig. \ref{fig:object_counts}). \textbf{Shard prefixes} (e.g., hash or date shards) to distribute keys across S3 partitions and avoid hot prefixes as datasets grow. \textbf{Keep frame–sized chunks} \((1,\cdot,\cdot)\) for frame–granularity reads and adjust only if the access unit changes. \textbf{Monitor tails on \texttt{lz4}}: slice \(p_{95}\) is heavier for Zarr+\texttt{lz4}, where prefer \texttt{zstd} for tighter latency envelopes or mitigate with concurrency and consolidated metadata (Fig. \ref{fig:slice_lz4_p95_mbb} and also see Fig. \ref{fig:object_counts}).


\subsection{TileDB}
\textbf{Manage fragment counts}: each write produces an immutable fragment, where many small fragments raise metadata work. Schedule \textbf{fragment consolidation} when counts grow to keep open/scan overhead low (Table \ref{tab:object_counts}). \textbf{Align tile extents} to the access unit (frame–aligned tiles for these arrays) to preserve long sequential reads. These practices sustain \textbf{scan dominance} observed across codecs (Figs. \ref{fig:full_gzip_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_lz4_gm_mbb}, \ref{fig:full_zstd_gm_mbb}) and the favorable throughput vs GETs/GiB posture (Fig. \ref{fig:cross_full_throughput_vs_gets}).

\subsection{HSDS (HDF5)}
\textbf{Account for REST fan–out} and \textbf{many small objects}: chunk and metadata requests add round–trips (Table \ref{tab:object_counts}). Provision and tune \textbf{server–side caches} (dataset/chunk caches) and concurrency to hide latency, right-size chunks to reduce request pressure where feasible. CloudWatch service latencies are stable across sessions, so speedups must come from reducing fan–out or improving caching, not expecting “faster S3” (Figs. \ref{fig:cw_firstbyte_grid}, \ref{fig:cw_total_grid}).

\subsection{ROOT/RNTuple}
\textbf{Batch page reads} and \textbf{avoid per–frame random access}: issue ranged reads and increase read windows so many small pages are fetched together. Prefer workflows that \textbf{scan clusters} over frame–random access for images. Remember that \textbf{few GETs do not imply speed}: single–object layout plus paging/decode keeps latency high despite low GETs/GiB (Tables \ref{tab:cross_slice_latency_vs_gets}, \ref{tab:slice_gzip_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_lz4_mbb}, \ref{tab:slice_zstd_mbb}).



\section{Future work}
The agenda below targets the highest-leverage uncertainties revealed by the results. Each item is derived from observed mechanisms and aims to either de-risk tail behavior or generalize beyond a single dataset, region, or client.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-client scaling and caches.} Test contention and gateway cache effects (HSDS/Zarr); report impact on GM and $p_{95}$ under load.
  \item \textbf{Consolidation strategies.} Evaluate Zarr metadata consolidation and TileDB fragment consolidation on GETs/GiB, open time, and tails.
  \item \textbf{Codec/filter and layout sweep.} Add Snappy, bitshuffle{+}zstd levels, ZFP; sweep chunk/tile sizes aligned to target access units.
  \item \textbf{Datasets and patterns.} Extend to sparse or multi-detector stacks; include batched/random windows and mixed read/write.
  \item \textbf{Platforms and cost surfaces.} Compare regions/clouds and on-prem PFS; model request+storage+egress cost surfaces for typical workloads.
  \item \textbf{Decision matrix v2.} Add weight-sensitivity visualization, per-site presets, and a one-page selection checklist.
  \item \textbf{Cold‑open and metadata experiments.} Quantify Zarr \texttt{consolidate\_metadata()} vs. unconsolidated stores and TileDB fragment consolidation on cold‑open time, GETs/GiB, and $p_{95}$ tails.
\end{itemize}
